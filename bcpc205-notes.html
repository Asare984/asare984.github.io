Week 1 — Overview of Marketing 1

Marketing is about identifying, anticipating and satisfying customer needs profitably. It is much more than advertising or selling.

Key points

  • AMA (2013): marketing is the activity, set of institutions and processes for creating, communicating, delivering and exchanging offerings that have value for customers, clients, partners and society.
  • CIM (UK): the management process responsible for identifying, anticipating and satisfying consumer requirements profitably.
  • Identifying and anticipating needs uses market research and strategic marketing planning. Satisfying needs uses product development, pricing, promotion, distribution and personal selling.
  • Nature of marketing: a concept, a business function, an umbrella term for many techniques, an ongoing process and a system.
  • Common wrong assumptions: marketing is advertising, marketing is selling, marketing is getting people to buy what they do not want.
  • Functions: distribution, financing, marketing information management, product/service management, promotion and selling.
  • Core ideas: needs (states of deprivation), wants (needs shaped by culture and personality), demands (wants backed by buying power).
  • Marketing myopia is focusing on existing wants and losing sight of the underlying need. Exchange is obtaining something by offering something in return. A market is the set of actual and potential buyers.

Week 2 — Overview of Marketing 2

This week covers how firms think about marketing: the five orientations, the marketing mix and building relationships.

Key points

  • Value proposition: the set of benefits a company promises to deliver to satisfy customers' needs.
  • Production concept: consumers favour products that are available and highly affordable.
  • Product concept: consumers favour the highest quality, performance and features, so the firm keeps improving the product.
  • Selling concept: consumers will not buy enough unless the firm does a large selling and promotion effort.
  • Marketing concept: organisational goals are achieved by knowing target customers' needs and delivering satisfaction better than competitors.
  • Societal marketing concept: balances consumer wants, company requirements, consumers' long-term interests and society's long-run interests.
  • Marketing mix (the 4 Ps): product, price, promotion, place. The integrated marketing program delivers the intended value to chosen customers.
  • Partner relationship management: working with partners inside and outside the company to bring greater value to customers.

Week 3 — The Marketing Environment

Every firm operates within an environment that creates opportunities and threats. Marketers must monitor it and respond.

Key points

  • Marketing environment: the actors and forces outside marketing that affect management's ability to build successful transactions with target customers.
  • Micro-environment (close to the firm): units in the firm, customers, suppliers, marketing intermediaries, competitors and publics.
  • Macro-environment (remote forces): socio-cultural, legal/political, economic, demographic, technological and natural environments.
  • Customer markets: consumer, business, reseller, institutional, government and international.
  • Marketing intermediaries: resellers, physical distribution firms, marketing services agencies and financial intermediaries.
  • Publics: financial, media, government, citizen action, local, general and internal publics.
  • Economic environment: income distribution, purchasing power and spending patterns. In tough times marketers use value-based marketing.
  • Technological environment: fast change, high R&D costs and increased regulation. Natural environment: shortages of raw materials, energy costs and pollution.
  • Firms either adapt to the environment or try to manage it.

Week 5 — Understanding Consumer Behaviour

Understanding how and why consumers buy lets marketers design offers and communication that fit.

Key points

  • Consumer behaviour: the buying behaviour of final consumers, meaning individuals and households buying for personal consumption.
  • Types of buying: routine response (low value, little thought), limited decision making, extensive decision making (durables) and impulse buying.
  • Decision process: problem recognition, information search, evaluation of alternatives, purchase decision, post-purchase evaluation.
  • Evoked set: the number of brands a consumer considers. Brand image: the beliefs a consumer holds about a brand.
  • Perceived risk strongly affects purchase decisions. Cognitive dissonance is discomfort after purchase.
  • Personal factors: demographics, situation and level of involvement.
  • Psychological factors: motivation, perception, personality.
  • Social factors: culture and sub-culture, roles and status, reference groups, family and social class.
  • Buying roles: initiator, influencer, decider, buyer and user.

Week 6 — Segmentation, Targeting and Positioning

Segmentation, targeting and positioning (STP) is how firms choose which customers to serve and how to be seen by them.

Key points

  • Segmentation: dividing a large, varied market into smaller groups with similar needs that can be reached efficiently.
  • Bases for consumer markets: geographic, demographic, psychographic and behavioural segmentation.
  • Requirements for effective segments: measurable, accessible, profitable, differentiable and actionable.
  • Benefits: precise market definition, better competitor analysis, quick response to change, efficient use of resources.
  • Target market: the set of buyers the firm decides to serve. Evaluate segment attractiveness and company fit.
  • Targeting strategies: undifferentiated (one offer for all), differentiated (separate offers for several segments), concentrated or niche (one or few segments), micromarketing (individuals or locations).
  • Positioning: the place a product holds in the minds of the target audience relative to competitors. Approaches: attributes, price/quality, competitors, application, user.
  • Repositioning is needed when needs change, new competitors appear, or technology changes.

Week 8 — Setting Product Strategy

The product is the heart of the marketing mix. This week covers classification, life cycle, portfolio and new product development.

Key points

  • Product: anything offered to a market for attention, acquisition, use or consumption that might satisfy a want or need.
  • Consumer goods: durable and non-durable. Convenience products (staples, impulse, emergency), shopping products, specialty products and unsought products.
  • Industrial products: capital items, materials and parts, supplies and services.
  • Product life cycle: introduction (low sales, no profit), growth (rapid acceptance), maturity (growth slows, intense competition), decline.
  • Ways to extend the life cycle: modify the product, change distribution, adjust price, run a promotion.
  • BCG matrix uses market growth rate and relative market share: stars, question marks, cash cows, dogs.
  • New product development: idea generation, idea screening, business analysis, product development, test marketing, commercialisation.
  • Reasons for product failure include overestimating market size, poor design, wrong timing, wrong price and weak promotion.

Week 9 — Pricing Strategies and Programmes

Price is the only element of the marketing mix that brings in revenue. Pricing decisions depend on objectives, costs, competitors and customers.

Key points

  • Price: the amount charged for a product or service, or the value customers exchange for its benefits.
  • Internal influences: marketing objectives, cost pattern, existing prices of the firm's other products.
  • External influences: competitors, elasticity of demand, suppliers, customers' motives and government policy.
  • Pricing objectives: income-oriented (target return, profit maximisation), sales-oriented (sales and market share growth), status-quo (meeting competition, non-price competition).
  • General strategies: low-price, market-price and high-price strategies.
  • Skimming: high initial price for a new product. Penetration: one low price for the whole market, used when demand is elastic.
  • Destroyer pricing: price cut to damage competitors or warn new entrants. Promotional pricing uses loss leaders.
  • Price competition suits firms that are low-cost producers and can respond quickly. Non-price competition stresses other parts of the mix.

Week 10 — Place and Marketing Channels

Place is about making products available in the right quantity and at the right place. Channels and physical distribution do this work.

Key points

  • Marketing channel: a set of interdependent organisations that make a product available for use or consumption.
  • Benefits of intermediaries: fewer contacts and transactions, lower storage costs, sorting and specialist skills.
  • Intermediaries: wholesalers, agents and brokers, and retailers (supermarkets, hypermarkets, department stores, discount stores, co-operative stores, mail order, franchises).
  • Franchise: permission to market a product in a specified area. The franchisor sells the franchise; the franchisee buys it.
  • Evaluate channels on economic performance, control and adaptability.
  • Intensive distribution uses as many outlets as possible; selective uses a few; exclusive uses one.
  • Vertical marketing systems: corporate (one ownership), administered (a dominant member coordinates), contractual (formal agreements).
  • Physical distribution: transport and storage. Transport modes: road, inland waterways, rail, air, pipelines and sea. Factors: cost, time, accessibility, reliability, traceability, capability.

Week 11 — Promotion

Promotion is how the firm communicates with the market to inform, persuade and remind.

Key points

  • Promotion: communication that informs, persuades and reminds potential buyers in order to influence an opinion or elicit a response.
  • Communication process: sender, encoding, message, channel, decoding, receiver and feedback, with noise as a barrier.
  • Promotional mix: advertising, personal selling, sales promotion and public relations.
  • Advertising: paid, non-personal presentation through mass media by an identified sponsor. Seven steps: target market, objectives, message, budget, media plan, execution, evaluation.
  • Personal selling steps: preparation, prospecting, pre-approach, presentation, handling objections, closing, post-sale follow-up.
  • Sales promotion types: consumer-oriented, trade-oriented, salesforce.
  • Public relations: building good relations with publics through favourable publicity and a good corporate image. Tools: press relations, product publicity, corporate communication, lobbying, counselling.

Week 12 — Building and Maintaining Customer Relationships

Today firms build long-term relationships with customers rather than chasing single sales.

Key points

  • CRM: the overall process of building and maintaining profitable customer relationships by delivering superior value and satisfaction.
  • Customer-perceived value: the difference between total customer value and total customer cost.
  • Customer satisfaction: how well the product's perceived performance matches expectations.
  • Selective relationship management targets fewer, more profitable customers. Customer-managed relationships are shaped by customers using digital technology.
  • Capabilities needed: data collection, marketing tools, customisation, customer service procedures and access channels.
  • Partner relationship management and the supply chain connect suppliers to final buyers.
  • Customer lifetime value is the value of all a customer's purchases over their lifetime. Share of customer is the portion of the customer's spending the firm gets. Customer equity is the total of all customers' lifetime values.
