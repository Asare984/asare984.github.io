Weeks 1-2 — What Is Law? Sources of Law in Ghana

Law is a set of rules that regulate people's conduct in society. In Ghana its sources are listed in Article 11 of the 1992 Constitution.

Key points

  • Law: a body of rules and regulations regulating the conduct and activities of people in a society. It is a mechanism of social control.
  • Article 11(1) lists the laws of Ghana: the Constitution; enactments by or under Parliament; orders, rules and regulations made under constitutional power; the existing law; and the common law.
  • Common law includes the common law rules, the rules of equity and customary law.
  • Equity: principles that supplement the common law where it would operate harshly. Maxims: 'He who comes to equity must come with clean hands'; 'He who seeks equity must do equity'.
  • Customary law is community specific, developed through usage, and must not be repugnant to natural justice or the Constitution.
  • Existing law: the written and unwritten laws of Ghana as they were before the 1992 Constitution came into force.
  • Bills: must be published in the Gazette at least 14 days before introduction to Parliament. Bills on chieftaincy go first to the National House of Chiefs.
  • Delegated legislation: rules made under Parliament's authority, for example by ministers.

Weeks 1-2 — The Court System, Litigation and ADR

Ghana's courts form a hierarchy. Alternatives to court, such as mediation and arbitration, are often faster and cheaper.

Key points

  • Supreme Court: final appellate court, with exclusive jurisdiction over interpretation and enforcement of the Constitution.
  • Court of Appeal: appellate only, normally a panel of three; appeals go to the Supreme Court.
  • High Court: one judge; original jurisdiction in civil and criminal matters; enforces fundamental human rights.
  • Regional Tribunals: special criminal jurisdiction, no civil jurisdiction.
  • Circuit Court: civil claims up to GH¢2,000,000; land and landlord/tenant cases; child custody.
  • District Court: largest number of courts; civil claims up to GH¢500,000. Family, Juvenile and Motor Courts are District Courts.
  • Litigation is slow and costly. ADR (negotiation, mediation, arbitration) is quicker and reduces case backlog.
  • Negotiation: the parties settle themselves. Mediation: a neutral facilitator helps the parties reach a voluntary agreement. Arbitration: a third party decides.
  • The Commercial Division of the High Court handles commercial disputes efficiently.

Weeks 3-4 — Introduction to the Law of Contract and Offer

A contract is an agreement the law will enforce. It starts with an offer.

Key points

  • Contract: an agreement, or promise, that the law will enforce.
  • Elements: offer, acceptance, consideration, intention to create legal relations and capacity.
  • Privity of contract: only the parties can enjoy rights or bear duties under the contract, with some exceptions.
  • Offer: a proposal showing willingness to be bound if accepted (NTHC Ltd v Antwi).
  • An invitation to treat is only an invitation to negotiate. Display of goods is an invitation to treat (Fisher v Bell; Pharmaceutical Society v Boots).
  • Termination of an offer: revocation (effective when received), rejection, counter-offer, lapse of time, death or insanity, destruction of subject matter.
  • A counter-offer rejects the original offer.

Week 4 — Acceptance and Consideration

A contract is formed when an offer is accepted and supported by consideration.

Key points

  • Acceptance: a final and unqualified assent to the terms of the offer. It may be express or implied from conduct.
  • Acceptance turns the offer into a contract and must be absolute.
  • If the offeror prescribes a method of acceptance, use it, or one that is as swift (Manchester Diocesan Council case).
  • The offeree must know of the offer.
  • Consideration: the price or inducement for a promise; a benefit to the promisor or a detriment to the promisee.
  • If consideration fails completely, there is no contract.

Week 5 — Intention to Create Legal Relations and Capacity

The parties must intend legal consequences and must have capacity to contract.

Key points

  • Intention to create legal relations: the parties intend their agreement to be enforceable.
  • Commercial agreements: presumed to be intended to create legal relations. Domestic agreements (husband and wife, parent and child) are presumed not to be. Presumptions can be rebutted.
  • Minors: the lecture says the contractual age in Ghana is 21, though other laws, such as the Children's Act 1998, set 18 for other purposes.
  • A contract between a minor and an adult is not binding on the minor but is binding on the adult, with exceptions.
  • Necessaries (Sale of Goods Act, 1962, Act 137, s.2(3)): goods suitable to the person's condition in life and actual requirements. Contracts for necessaries bind a minor.
  • Beneficial contracts of service, such as apprenticeships, bind a minor. Some contracts are voidable and binding until the minor avoids them.
  • A company has the powers of a natural person of full capacity.

Week 6 — Mistake and Misrepresentation

Some factors make consent defective and affect the validity of a contract. These are vitiating factors.

Key points

  • Mistake: a party contracts on terms, with a person, or for a subject matter they did not intend.
  • Unilateral mistake (one party): generally does not affect the contract.
  • Common mistake (both parties make the same mistake): if the subject matter does not exist, the contract is void (Bell v Lever Brothers). Mistake as to quality generally does not make a contract void (Magee v Pennine Insurance).
  • Misrepresentation: a false statement of fact made to induce a contract but not forming part of it.
  • Fraudulent misrepresentation: made knowingly, without belief in its truth, or recklessly. The contract is voidable (Clarke v Dickson).
  • Negligent misrepresentation: made carelessly in breach of a duty. Innocent misrepresentation: made honestly; the misled party may rescind.

Week 7 — Duress, Undue Influence and Illegality

Pressure, unfair influence and illegality can stop a contract from being enforced.

Key points

  • Duress: consent obtained by improper pressure or threats. The contract is voidable.
  • Undue influence: an equitable doctrine where one person uses influence over another to get them to contract on disadvantageous terms.
  • Presumed undue influence can arise where there is a relationship of trust and confidence.
  • Illegality: the subject matter, purpose or performance contravenes the law.
  • Contracts against public policy harm the public interest.
  • Restraint of trade: enforceable only if reasonable and just.
  • Void contracts have no effect from the start. Voidable contracts are valid until set aside.

Week 8 — Discharge of Contracts

A contract may end by performance, agreement, frustration or breach.

Key points

  • Discharge: parties are relieved of their obligations.
  • Performance: must be complete and exact. Exceptions: divisible contracts, accepted part performance, prevention by the other party, substantial performance.
  • Agreement: the parties agree to end or replace the contract.
  • Frustration: events beyond the parties' control make performance impossible or impracticable (Taylor v Caldwell, hire of a music hall destroyed by fire).
  • Breach: a breach always allows an action for damages; a fundamental breach discharges the innocent party.
  • Anticipatory breach: a party shows before the time that they will not perform (Frost v Knight).

Weeks 9-10 — Agency

Agency allows one person to act for another and bind them in contracts.

Key points

  • Agency: a relationship in which an agent is appointed to represent a principal and can make contracts that bind the principal. It is a fiduciary relationship.
  • Creation: by agreement (express or implied), by implication and by necessity.
  • No formality is needed to appoint an agent, except that an agent must be appointed by deed to execute a deed.
  • Agency of necessity: agent in control of the principal's property; impossible to get instructions; genuine emergency; agent acts bona fide.
  • Express authority is given by agreement. Implied authority covers what is reasonable, incidental and necessary (Ryan v Pilkington).
  • Agent's duties: fiduciary duty, skill and care, and following instructions.
  • Principal's duties: includes indemnifying the agent.

Weeks 11-12 — Sale of Goods

The Sale of Goods Act, 1962 (Act 137) governs contracts where goods are sold for a price.

Key points

  • Section 1(1): a contract where the seller agrees to transfer property in goods to the buyer for a price, wholly or partly in money.
  • Differences: barter is goods for goods; a gift is free; bailment passes possession but not ownership.
  • Specific goods (s.5) are identified and agreed at the time of contract. Unascertained goods are not. Future goods do not yet exist.
  • Nemo dat (s.28): a non-owner generally cannot pass a better title than he has.
  • Buyer in possession: a second buyer in good faith can get good title.
  • Sale or return: ownership passes when the buyer approves, adopts the goods or keeps them too long.
  • Seller's duties: right quantity (s.14), time of delivery (s.16), right to sell (s.10), goods by sample (s.8(2)), quality and fitness (s.13).
  • Buyer's duties (ss.21-22): accept delivery and pay the price.
