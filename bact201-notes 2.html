Week 1 — Introduction to Accounting

The big ideaAccounting is the language of business. It records what a business owns and owes, measures its profit, and communicates this to the people who need to know.

What accounting is

Accounting is the process of identifying, measuring and communicating economic information so that users can make informed judgements and decisions. It involves recording, classifying and summarising transactions in money terms.

Example: when a shop sells goods, accounting records the sale, the cost of the goods, and the profit.

A little history

Modern accounting owes its origin to Luca Pacioli, an Italian, who in 1494 published a book describing the double-entry system. He is called the father of accounting.

Remember: the double-entry method you learn is over 500 years old.

Aims of accounting

To keep systematic records of transactions, to protect the business's property, to find out the profit or loss, to show the financial position, and to supply information for decisions.

Example: a trader wants to know whether this month's work made a profit.

Financial statements

A full set contains five parts: the statement of financial position (balance sheet), the statement of comprehensive income (profit or loss), the statement of changes in equity, the cash flow statement, and notes explaining the policies used.

Tip: balance sheet = what you own and owe on a date; income statement = profit over a period.

Who sets the rules

Standard setters include the GNASB (Ghana National Accounting Standards Board), the FASB in the US, the ASB in the UK, and the IASB internationally.

Remember: standards make different businesses' accounts comparable.

Qualities of useful information

Fundamental: relevance (it can influence decisions) and faithful representation (it truthfully shows what happened). Enhancing: comparability, verifiability, timeliness and understandability.

Example: accounts that arrive a year late fail the timeliness test.

Who uses accounts

Owners, management, lenders, investors, employees, suppliers, customers, tax authorities and auditors.

Example: a bank reads your accounts before approving a loan.

Branches of accounting

Financial accounting serves outsiders and looks at the past. Management accounting serves managers inside the firm and looks at the future (budgets, costs). Tax accounting deals with tax rules.

Example: preparing next year's budget is management accounting.

Quick recap

  • Pacioli, 1494, double entry
  • Five statements
  • Qualities: relevance and faithful representation first
  • Financial = outsiders, past; management = insiders, future

Week 2 — Business Documents

The big ideaEvery transaction starts with a document that proves it happened. These source documents are the evidence for the entries in the books.

Why source documents matter

They give the details of each transaction, provide evidence for the auditor, and prove income and spending to tax authorities. No document, no proof.

Example: keeping the receipt when you buy goods for the business.

Purchase order

A purchase order tells a supplier exactly what goods you want to buy. It is a request, not yet a purchase, so ordinarily nothing is recorded in the books until goods arrive.

Example: you order 50 bags of flour from a supplier.

Invoice

An invoice shows the details of goods sold on credit: what, how many, price and total. It is a sales invoice to the seller and a purchase invoice to the buyer.

Example: the supplier sends an invoice for GH¢2,000 due in 30 days.

Receipt

A receipt is proof that money has been received.

Example: the shop gives you a receipt when you pay cash.

Cheque

A cheque is a written order to a bank to pay a stated sum on demand to a named person, their order or the bearer.

Example: paying a supplier by cheque.

Payment voucher

A payment voucher is an authorised document supporting a payment. It is the source for the credit side of the cash book. A payment can only be made after the voucher is authorised.

Remember: it controls who can approve spending.

Journal voucher

A journal voucher does the work of the journal proper, for entries that do not pass through other books, and needs authorisation.

Example: correcting an error.

Bank statement and bank advice

The bank statement lists everything that went in and out of your account. A bank advice slip tells you of items such as standing orders, interest and charges.

Example: bank charges you did not know about appear on the statement.

Quick recap

  • Source documents prove transactions
  • Order, invoice, receipt, cheque, voucher
  • Payment voucher is source for cash book credit side

Week 3 — Accounting Concepts and Conventions

The big ideaAccounting concepts and conventions are the ground rules that accountants follow so accounts are fair and comparable.

Going concern

Assume the business will continue operating in the foreseeable future. So assets are valued as items in use, not at what they would fetch if sold off in a hurry.

Example: a machine is valued on its cost less depreciation, not its scrap value.

Consistency

Once you choose a method, use it the same way every year, so results can be compared.

Example: if you depreciate vehicles by straight line, do not switch to another method every year.

Accruals (matching)

Record income when it is earned and expenses when they are incurred, not when cash moves. Profit is revenue less the expenses used to earn that revenue.

Example: electricity used in December but paid in January is a December expense.

Prudence

Be cautious: do not overstate profits or assets, and recognise losses as soon as they are likely. But do not deliberately understate profit either.

Example: provide for a customer who probably will not pay.

Substance over form

Record the economic reality of a transaction, not just its legal form.

Example: a long lease that is really a purchase is treated as an asset.

Business entity

The business is separate from its owner. The owner's private spending is not a business expense.

Example: a trader buying groceries for home from the till records it as drawings.

Money measurement

Only things that can be measured in money are recorded.

Example: the skill of your staff is valuable, but it is not in the books.

Realisation and disclosure

Realisation: recognise profit when goods are delivered. Disclosure: give all information users need.

Example: a pending lawsuit should be disclosed in the notes.

Quick recap

  • Going concern, consistency, accruals, prudence
  • Substance over form, business entity, money measurement
  • Realisation, disclosure

Week 4 — Systems of Recording and the Accounting Equation

The big ideaThe accounting equation is the foundation of double entry. At all times: Assets = Capital + Liabilities.

The three elements

Assets are what the business owns (cash, stock, equipment). Liabilities are what it owes to outsiders (loans, suppliers). Capital is what is owed to the owner: the owner's investment plus profits less drawings.

Example: you start a business with GH¢50,000 cash. Assets 50,000 = Capital 50,000 + Liabilities 0.

Single and double entry

Single entry records only one side of a transaction. Double entry records both: every transaction has a debit and an equal credit, which is why the equation always balances.

Remember: double entry catches mistakes because the two sides must match.

How transactions change the equation

Every transaction changes at least two items but keeps the equation in balance.

See the worked example below.

Day books

These are books of original entry for repeated transactions. The sales day book records credit sales from sales invoices. The purchases day book records credit purchases. Return inwards are goods customers send back.

Example: a customer returns damaged goods, reducing sales.

Worked example: Ama's business

  1. Start: Cash 50,000. Assets 50,000 = Capital 50,000 + Liabilities 0
  2. 1) Borrows GH¢20,000 from bank: Assets 70,000 = Capital 50,000 + Liabilities 20,000
  3. 2) Buys stock GH¢3,000 on credit: Assets 73,000 = Capital 50,000 + Liabilities 23,000
  4. 3) Pays supplier GH¢3,000 cash: Assets 70,000 = Capital 50,000 + Liabilities 20,000
  5. 4) Pays rent GH¢2,000: Assets 68,000 = Capital 48,000 + Liabilities 20,000
  6. Each time, the two sides are equal

Quick recap

  • Assets = Capital + Liabilities
  • Double entry: every debit has a credit
  • Day books record credit transactions

Week 4-5 — The Journal, Capital and Revenue Expenditure

The big ideaThe journal is where each transaction is first written in double-entry form before it is posted to the ledger.

Two stages of recording

Stage 1: record the transaction in the journal (the book of original entry). Stage 2: post it to the ledger accounts.

Remember: the journal is the diary; the ledger is the filing system.

A journal entry

Each entry shows the date, the account to debit, the account to credit, the amounts, and a short narration beginning with 'Being'.

Example: Dr Cash 5,000, Cr Capital 5,000. Being capital introduced in cash.

Common entries

Capital introduced in cash: debit Cash, credit Capital. Furniture bought on credit: debit Furniture, credit the supplier. Goods sold for cash: debit Cash, credit Sales. Owner takes cash: debit Drawings, credit Cash.

Rule of thumb: debit what comes in or what is used up; credit what goes out or what is owed.

Capital and revenue expenditure

Capital expenditure buys or improves assets that last more than one year; it goes on the balance sheet. Revenue expenditure is spending used up within the year; it goes to the profit and loss account.

Examples: buying a van is capital. Fuel and repairs are revenue.

Why the difference matters

If you wrongly treat a capital item as an expense, you understate profit and assets. If you treat an expense as capital, you overstate them.

Example: painting an old building is revenue; building an extension is capital.

Capital and revenue receipts

Capital receipts are one-off, such as owner capital or a long-term loan. Revenue receipts come from normal trading.

Example: sales income is a revenue receipt.

Quick recap

  • Journal first, ledger second
  • Narration starts with 'Being'
  • Capital = lasting asset; revenue = used up this year

Week 5 — The Ledger and the Cash Book

The big ideaThe ledger is the main book where all transactions are grouped by account. The cash book is a special ledger for money.

Debit and credit rules

Debit when: assets increase, expenses increase, drawings increase. Credit when: liabilities increase, capital increases, income increases. To reduce an item, do the opposite.

Example: when you pay rent in cash, debit Rent (expense up) and credit Cash (asset down).

T accounts

An account is drawn like a letter T: debit on the left, credit on the right.

Example: the Cash account receives debits when cash comes in and credits when it goes out.

Balancing an account

Total both sides, find the difference, and write it on the smaller side as the balance carried down (c/d). Bring it down (b/d) on the opposite side to start the next period.

See the worked example below.

The cash book

The cash book records all cash and bank transactions. It is both a book of original entry and a ledger account. It can have one column (cash), two columns (cash and discount) or three (discount, cash, bank).

Example: a three-column cash book for a shop with a bank account.

Discounts

Discount allowed is a reduction given to customers who pay early; it goes on the debit side of the cash book. Discount received is a reduction from suppliers; it goes on the credit side.

Tip: allowed = to customers = debit; received = from suppliers = credit.

Contra entries

A contra entry moves money between cash and bank, for example depositing cash. It appears on both sides of the cash book.

Example: paying GH¢2,000 cash into the bank.

Worked example: Cash account

  1. Debit side: Capital 50,000; Sales 8,000. Total 58,000
  2. Credit side: Purchases 12,000; Rent 3,000. Total 15,000
  3. Difference = 58,000 - 15,000 = 43,000
  4. Balance c/d 43,000 on the credit side, brought down b/d 43,000 on the debit side
  5. Cash on hand is GH¢43,000

Quick recap

  • Debit assets and expenses up; credit liabilities, capital, income up
  • Balance: c/d then b/d
  • Cash book is book of original entry and ledger
  • Discount allowed = debit; received = credit

Week 6 — The Trial Balance and Errors

The big ideaA trial balance lists all the ledger balances at a date to check that total debits equal total credits.

What it is for

It checks the arithmetic of double entry, summarises the ledger, and is the starting point for final accounts.

Remember: if debits do not equal credits, there is an error somewhere.

Errors it will NOT show

The totals can still agree even with errors. Omission: a transaction left out entirely. Commission: posted to the wrong account of the same type. Principle: posted to the wrong class (for example an expense treated as an asset). Reversal: debit and credit swapped. Compensating: two errors of equal amounts cancel out. Original entry: wrong amount posted on both sides.

Example: electricity bill posted to the water account (commission).

Errors it WILL show

One-sided posting, wrong addition, or different amounts on the two sides. These cause the totals to differ.

Example: debit of 500 posted but credit left out.

Suspense account

When the trial balance does not agree, the difference is put in a temporary suspense account until the errors are found and corrected.

See the worked example below.

A caution

A trial balance that agrees does not prove there are no errors.

Remember: always check beyond the totals.

Worked example: suspense account

  1. Total debits = GH¢85,400
  2. Total credits = GH¢84,900
  3. Credits are 500 short, so credit Suspense 500 to make both sides equal
  4. When the error is found (for example a 500 credit omitted), debit Suspense and credit the correct account to clear it

Quick recap

  • TB checks debits = credits
  • Errors of omission, commission, principle, reversal, compensating, original entry hide from TB
  • Difference goes to suspense

Weeks 7-8 — Petty Cash and Bank Reconciliation

The big ideaPetty cash handles small payments, and a bank reconciliation explains why your cash book and the bank statement show different balances.

Petty cash and the imprest system

A petty cashier handles small day-to-day payments. Under the imprest system, the cashier starts with a fixed float and is reimbursed periodically with exactly the amount spent, restoring the float to its original level.

Example: float GH¢400. After spending GH¢250, the cashier receives GH¢250 to bring the cash back to GH¢400.

Petty cash book

Payments are recorded in analysis columns (for example postage, travel, stationery) so it is easy to total each type.

Example: 'Stationery 30' goes in the stationery column.

Why bank reconciliation

The cash book (your record) and the bank statement (the bank's record) often differ. A reconciliation explains the difference and helps detect errors and fraud.

Example: your cash book says GH¢4,780 but the bank says GH¢4,500.

Reasons for differences

Timing: cheques you issued (unpresented cheques) not yet paid by the bank, and money you banked (uncleared lodgements) not yet credited. Items the bank knows and you do not: bank charges, standing orders, direct debits, direct receipts. Errors by either party.

Tip: items in your cash book but not yet at the bank are timing differences; items on the statement but not in your cash book need adjusting.

Steps

First, update the cash book for items on the statement that are missing (charges, standing orders, direct receipts). Then reconcile: bank statement balance + uncleared lodgements - unpresented cheques = adjusted cash book balance.

See the worked example below.

Worked example: bank reconciliation

  1. Cash book balance (debit) = 5,000
  2. Adjust: bank charges -120, standing order -300, direct dividend +200
  3. Adjusted cash book = 5,000 - 120 - 300 + 200 = 4,780
  4. Bank statement balance = 4,500
  5. Add uncleared lodgements +880, deduct unpresented cheques -600
  6. Reconciled = 4,500 + 880 - 600 = 4,780. Both agree

Quick recap

  • Imprest = fixed float restored by amount spent
  • Unpresented cheques and uncleared lodgements are timing items
  • Adjust cash book first, then reconcile

Weeks 9-10 — Depreciation and Disposal of Fixed Assets

The big ideaDepreciation spreads the cost of a long-lasting asset over the years it is used, so each year bears a fair share of the cost.

What depreciation is

Fixed assets are bought for use, not for resale. They lose value through wear and tear, the passage of time and obsolescence. Depreciation is the allocation of the cost of the asset over its useful life, charged as an expense each year.

Example: a van bought for GH¢50,000 is used for five years, so each year takes a share of the cost.

Why we charge it

To match the cost of using the asset with the income it helps earn, to show a true profit, and to show assets at a fair value on the balance sheet.

Remember: without depreciation, profit is overstated.

Straight-line method

Same charge every year: (cost - scrap value) / estimated useful life.

Example: cost 50,000, scrap 5,000, life 5 years. (50,000 - 5,000) / 5 = GH¢9,000 a year.

Reducing balance method

A fixed percentage of the net book value each year, so the charge falls over time.

See the table below.

Net book value (NBV)

NBV = cost - accumulated depreciation. It is the asset's value in the books.

Example: after 2 years of straight-line, NBV = 50,000 - 18,000 = 32,000.

Disposal of a fixed asset

When you sell an asset, compare the sale price with the NBV. If proceeds are more than NBV, there is a profit; if less, a loss.

Example: cost 20,000, accumulated depreciation 12,000, so NBV = 8,000. Sold for 9,500: profit 1,500. Sold for 6,000: loss 2,000.

Worked example: reducing balance at 20%, cost GH¢50,000

  1. Year 1: 20% x 50,000 = 10,000; NBV = 40,000
  2. Year 2: 20% x 40,000 = 8,000; NBV = 32,000
  3. Year 3: 20% x 32,000 = 6,400; NBV = 25,600
  4. Notice the charge falls each year

Quick recap

  • SL = (cost - scrap) / life
  • Reducing balance = % x NBV
  • NBV = cost - accumulated depreciation
  • Disposal: proceeds vs NBV

Weeks 11-12 — Final Accounts

The big ideaFinal accounts show whether the business made a profit and what it owns and owes at the year end.

The components

The statement of profit or loss (income statement), the statement of financial position (balance sheet), the cash flow statement, the statement of changes in equity and the notes.

Remember: the trading account is the first part of the profit and loss account.

The trading account

It finds the gross profit: sales minus the cost of goods sold. Cost of goods sold = opening stock + purchases - purchases returns + direct expenses (like carriage inwards) - closing stock.

See the worked example below.

The profit and loss account

Net profit = gross profit + other income - expenses (wages, rent, insurance, depreciation and so on).

Example: gross profit 18,000 less expenses 9,000 gives net profit 9,000.

Adjustments

Accruals are expenses owed but not yet paid; add them. Prepayments are paid in advance for next period; deduct them. Depreciation is an expense.

Example: insurance paid 500, of which 50 is prepaid, so the expense is 450.

Closing stock and drawings

Closing stock is deducted in the trading account and is shown as a current asset on the balance sheet. Drawings are not expenses; they reduce capital.

Remember: owner's withdrawals never reduce profit.

Worked example: Fiati's trading account

  1. Sales 60,000 less sales returns 10,000 = net sales 50,000
  2. Opening stock 10,000 + purchases 40,000 - purchases returns 3,000 = 47,000
  3. Less closing stock 15,000 = cost of goods sold 32,000
  4. Gross profit = 50,000 - 32,000 = 18,000
  5. Expenses: wages 5,000 + rent 2,000 + insurance 450 + depreciation 1,550 = 9,000
  6. Net profit = 18,000 - 9,000 = 9,000

Quick recap

  • Gross profit = net sales - cost of goods sold
  • Net profit = gross profit + other income - expenses
  • Prepayments deducted, accruals added
  • Drawings are not an expense
