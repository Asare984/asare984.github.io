Week 1 — What Is an Information System?

The big ideaAn information system is everything a business uses to turn raw facts into useful information: the equipment, the programs, the data, the people and the steps they follow.

Data versus information

Data are raw facts on their own, like numbers and names. Information is data that has been organised so it means something and helps you decide.

Example: the numbers 45, 67 and 82 are data. 'The class average is 64.7 and the highest mark is 82' is information.

The five components

Every information system has five parts that work together. Hardware is the physical equipment you can touch. Software is the programs that tell the hardware what to do. Data is the facts being stored and processed. People are those who use and run the system. Process is the series of steps followed to get a job done.

Example: the UPSA student portal. Hardware: servers and your phone. Software: the portal itself. Data: your results and fees. People: students and staff. Process: log in, pay fees, register courses, view results.

Why businesses use them

An information system helps a business collect facts quickly, store them safely and use them to make better decisions with fewer mistakes.

Example: a provision shop that records every sale on a mobile app can see which items sell fastest and when to restock, instead of guessing.

The mainframe era (late 1950s to 1960s)

The first business computers were huge, expensive machines kept in special rooms. Only big organisations could afford them, and they mainly did calculations. By the late 1960s, MRP systems appeared to help factories plan materials.

Example: a bank in the 1960s using one giant computer to process thousands of accounts.

The personal computer era (from 1975)

In 1975 the Altair 8800 was announced as the first microcomputer. It was small and cheap enough for individuals and small firms, and Microsoft Windows later made PCs easy to use.

Example: a small trader using a PC with a spreadsheet to keep accounts instead of a paper book.

The client-server era (mid 1980s)

Businesses began connecting their PCs in networks so staff could share files and printers. A central computer called a server provided services to the connected computers (the clients). The first ERP systems ran on this model.

Example: an office where all staff computers use one shared server for files and a shared printer.

The Web and e-commerce era

The Internet was invented in 1969 but was limited to universities and the military. In 1989 Tim Berners-Lee created the World Wide Web, a simple way to share information over the Internet. The late 1990s saw the dot-com boom, as firms rushed to sell online.

Example: buying a book from an online shop instead of visiting a bookstore.

Web 2.0, mobile and the cloud

Web 2.0 means users create and share content, such as blogs and social media. The post-PC world brought smartphones and tablets. Cloud computing means your applications and files live on the Internet instead of one computer.

Example: writing a document in Google Docs on your phone and opening it later on a laptop.

The Internet of Things

Everyday objects, such as fridges, cars and farm sensors, now connect to the Internet and share data automatically.

Example: a sensor in a farm field that reports soil moisture to the farmer's phone.

Quick recap

  • IS = hardware + software + data + people + process
  • Data are raw facts; information is useful, organised data
  • Eras: mainframe, PC (1975), client-server, Web (1989), Web 2.0 and cloud, Internet of Things

Week 2 — Hardware

The big ideaHardware is every physical part of a computer system. Learning how it works helps you choose the right device and understand why computers are fast or slow.

The CPU, the brain of the computer

The Central Processing Unit does the thinking. It follows instructions, does calculations and controls the other parts. Inside, the control unit directs traffic and the arithmetic logic unit does the maths. Its speed is measured in gigahertz (GHz), which means billions of cycles per second.

Example: a 3 GHz processor can do about 3 billion basic cycles every second, so it opens programs faster than a 1 GHz one.

Primary storage (memory)

Primary storage holds the data the computer is working on right now. It is very fast but temporary: when the power goes off, the contents disappear (this is called volatile). It comes in layers: registers (tiny and inside the CPU), cache memory (small and very fast), and RAM (the main working memory).

Example: when you type an assignment, the text sits in RAM. If the laptop shuts off before you save, you lose it.

Secondary storage

Secondary storage keeps data permanently, even when the power is off (nonvolatile). It holds much more than RAM and is cheaper per gigabyte, but it is slower to read. Examples are hard drives, SSDs, flash drives and memory cards.

Example: saving your assignment to a flash drive so it is still there tomorrow.

How storage is measured

The smallest unit is a bit (a 0 or a 1). A byte is 8 bits and holds one character. Then come kilobyte, megabyte, gigabyte and terabyte, each about a thousand times bigger than the last.

Example: a phone with 64 GB storage can hold far more photos than one with 16 GB.

Input, output and peripherals

Input devices send data in (keyboard, mouse, scanner, microphone). Output devices give results out (screen, printer, speakers). A peripheral is any extra device you connect to the computer.

Example: connecting a printer to your laptop to print your CV.

Types of computing device

Personal computers are desktops. Portable computers are laptops, notebooks and netbooks (a netbook is small and depends on the network). Smartphones and tablets are small touch devices that now do much of what computers do.

Example: many students use smartphones to access the UPSA portal.

Trends in hardware

Devices keep getting smaller and more portable (miniaturisation). Cloud computing reduces how much storage you need on the device itself. The Internet of Things connects billions of devices that each have a unique network address.

Example: a smartwatch that tracks your steps and sends the data to your phone.

Quick recap

  • CPU = brain, speed in GHz
  • Primary storage (registers, cache, RAM) is fast and volatile
  • Secondary storage is permanent, larger and cheaper
  • 1 byte = 8 bits
  • Peripherals are extra connected devices

Week 2 — Software

The big ideaSoftware is the set of instructions that tells hardware what to do. Without software, a computer is just a box of parts.

Two main kinds of software

System software runs the computer itself (the operating system and utilities). Application software lets you do tasks you care about, like writing, calculating or playing games.

Example: Windows or Android is system software. Microsoft Word or WhatsApp is application software.

The operating system

The operating system manages the hardware, gives you a screen you can interact with (the user interface), and looks after files and applications.

Example: when you tap the WhatsApp icon, the operating system finds the app, loads it into memory and shows it on the screen.

Productivity and other application software

Productivity software helps with office work: word processors, spreadsheets, presentations and databases. Utility software maintains the computer (antivirus, backup). Programming software is used to create other software.

Example: a student using a spreadsheet to calculate their GPA.

Network operating systems and ERP

A network operating system runs on a server and manages data, users, groups and security for a whole network. It is stable and centralised but can be costly. Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) software brings an organisation's information, such as finance, HR and stock, under one centralised system.

Example: a company where the stores, accounts and sales departments all use one ERP, so a sale instantly updates stock and accounts.

Cloud computing

Cloud software runs on the Internet instead of your own computer. The advantage is that there is nothing to install and you can reach it anywhere. The disadvantage is that your data sits on someone else's server.

Example: Google Drive.

Virtualisation and open-source

Virtualisation uses software to imitate a computer, so one physical machine can act like several. Open-source software has its source code freely available for anyone to use and improve.

Example: Linux and Firefox are open-source.

Security: viruses, worms and piracy

A virus is a harmful program that spreads from device to device, usually when someone opens an infected file. A worm spreads on its own, usually without anyone clicking anything. Piracy means copying software without the owner's permission, which is illegal. Protect yourself with antivirus software, a firewall, and regular updates.

Example: an email attachment from a stranger that installs a virus when opened.

Quick recap

  • System software runs the computer; application software does your tasks
  • OS manages hardware, files, user interface
  • Cloud = software and files on the Internet
  • Worms spread by themselves; viruses need a user action
  • Piracy is illegal copying of software

Week 3 — Networking and Communication

The big ideaA network connects computers so they can share data and talk to each other. The Internet is the biggest network of all.

How the Internet began

After the Soviet Union launched Sputnik in 1957, the United States created ARPA to lead in technology. ARPA built ARPANET, the first network and the ancestor of the Internet. A shared set of rules called TCP/IP later let different networks connect.

Example: TCP/IP is like a common language that lets a phone and a server understand each other.

Packets

Data is chopped into small pieces called packets. Each packet travels across the network on its own route, and the pieces are put back together at the other end.

Example: sending a photo on WhatsApp splits it into many packets that rejoin on your friend's phone.

Network devices

A hub connects devices and sends data to everyone. A switch is smarter: it sends data only to the device it is meant for. A router connects different networks, such as your home network to the Internet. A modem (modulator-demodulator) converts signals so they can travel over phone or cable lines.

Example: the Wi-Fi box at home is usually a modem and router in one.

Connection types

Broadband is an Internet connection of at least 256,000 bits per second. Wi-Fi turns an Internet signal into radio waves your devices pick up. Bluetooth (created in 1994) connects nearby devices. VoIP sends voice calls over the Internet.

Example: WhatsApp calls use VoIP.

Sizes of networks

A LAN (local area network) covers a small area like an office or campus. A WAN (wide area network) covers a large area, such as a company's branches in different cities.

Example: UPSA's campus network is a LAN.

Client-server, intranet and extranet

In client-server networks, a server provides services and clients use them. An intranet is a private network inside one organisation. An extranet is a private network opened securely to outsiders such as suppliers.

Example: a company intranet for staff notices, and an extranet for suppliers to check orders.

Internet versus the World Wide Web

The Internet is the global network of networks. The Web is just one service that runs on it: web pages opened in a browser. Email and video calls are other services.

Example: the Internet is the road system; the Web is one kind of vehicle that uses it.

Quick recap

  • ARPANET was the start; TCP/IP is the common language
  • Data travels in packets
  • Switch targets a device; hub sends to all; router connects networks
  • LAN is small area; WAN is wide area
  • Intranet = private inside; extranet = shared with chosen outsiders

Week 4 — Types of Information Systems

The big ideaDifferent people in an organisation need different information, so there are different types of information system for each level.

Three levels of an organisation

The operational level does day-to-day work. The management (tactical) level monitors and controls. The strategic level, the top, sets long-term direction. Each level needs its own kind of system.

Example: a cashier, a branch manager and the CEO of a supermarket chain.

Transaction processing system (TPS)

A TPS records and processes the routine, everyday transactions of a business and checks that they are valid. It can work in batches (collected and processed later) or online (processed immediately).

Example: a cashier scanning items at the till, payroll, or a hotel booking system.

Management information system (MIS)

An MIS takes the data from the TPS and turns it into routine reports for middle managers, so they can monitor performance.

Example: a weekly sales report by branch.

Decision support system (DSS)

A DSS helps managers make complex decisions that are not routine, often by testing 'what if' questions.

Example: a manager testing what would happen to profit if prices rise by 10%.

Executive information system (EIS)

An EIS is a special DSS for top executives. It shows key performance indicators on a simple dashboard.

Example: a CEO's screen showing total sales, profit and cash position at a glance.

Knowledge work systems and office automation systems

Knowledge work systems support experts like engineers and designers who create new knowledge. Office automation systems help data workers be more productive, for example with word processing, email and scheduling.

Example: an architect using design software (KWS), or a secretary using email and calendar tools (OAS).

Expert systems

An expert system copies the decision-making of a human expert in one narrow area. It has a knowledge base (facts and rules), an inference engine (the part that reasons) and a user interface.

Example: a medical system that suggests possible illnesses from symptoms.

Quick recap

  • TPS = routine transactions
  • MIS = routine reports for middle managers
  • DSS = complex, non-routine decisions
  • EIS = DSS for top executives
  • Expert system = knowledge base + inference engine + user interface

Week 4 — Globalisation and the Digital Divide

The big ideaTechnology has made the world feel smaller, but it has not reached everyone equally. That gap is called the digital divide.

What globalisation means

Globalisation is the growing connection of goods, services and culture between countries. Information technology is its engine because it lets people and firms communicate instantly across the world.

Example: a Ghanaian company selling to customers in Europe through a website.

Castells and Friedman

Manuel Castells described a 'network society': an economy able to work as a single unit in real time around the planet. Thomas Friedman, in The World Is Flat (2005), described three stages: Globalisation 1.0 (1492 to about 1800, driven by countries), 2.0 (driven by companies) and 3.0 (driven by individuals).

Example: today a single freelancer can serve clients in many countries.

Benefits and challenges for firms

Firms can find skilled people and cheaper labour anywhere and reach new markets. Challenges include differences in infrastructure, labour laws and legal rules, and cybersecurity.

Example: a firm hiring programmers abroad but needing to follow that country's labour laws.

The digital divide

The digital divide is the gap between people who have access to technology and the Internet and those who do not. It exists between countries and within them, for example between cities and villages.

Example: a student in a village without electricity or data cannot use the same online resources as a city student.

Nielsen's three stages

Jakob Nielsen described three divides: economic (who can afford access), usability (who has the skills to use it) and empowerment (who can use it to improve their life). The first-level divide is access and infrastructure, the second is digital skills, and the third is the quality and usefulness of content.

Example: having a smartphone but not knowing how to search effectively is the second-level divide.

One Laptop per Child

This project aimed to give low-cost laptops to children in developing countries so they could learn. It struggled because of cultural differences, corruption and competition.

Remember: giving a device alone does not close the divide. People also need training, power and content.

Quick recap

  • Globalisation = connection of goods, services and culture
  • Digital divide = access gap
  • Nielsen: economic, usability, empowerment
  • Friedman: 1.0 countries, 2.0 companies, 3.0 individuals

Week 5 — Artificial Intelligence in Business and Academia

The big ideaArtificial intelligence (AI) is when machines do things that normally need human intelligence, such as learning, understanding language and making decisions.

Where AI came from

The term was coined at a Dartmouth conference in 1956. Alan Turing was an early pioneer. There were periods called AI winters, such as 1974 to 1980, when funding dried up because AI promised more than it could deliver.

Example: early AI could play simple games but could not understand speech.

How AI works

AI learns from data. Programmers write algorithms (step-by-step instructions) that let the machine find patterns in huge amounts of data and improve with practice.

Example: a spam filter learns what spam looks like from thousands of marked emails.

The four types of AI

Reactive AI only responds to the present with fixed rules and has no memory. Limited memory AI uses past data to improve, such as self-driving features. Theory of mind AI would understand people's thoughts and feelings; it is still being developed. Self-aware AI would have its own consciousness; it is only a concept.

Example: a chess program is reactive; a voice assistant that learns your accent uses limited memory.

Benefits in business

AI gives faster and more personal customer service, takes over boring repetitive tasks, and helps managers make better decisions from large amounts of data.

Example: a chatbot answering customers at 2 a.m.

Risks and concerns

Workers may fear losing their jobs and mistrust AI. AI can make mistakes if its data or training is poor. It can also be expensive to build, and it raises ethical questions.

Example: an AI hiring tool that unfairly rejects candidates because it learned from biased old data.

Quick recap

  • AI named in 1956 at Dartmouth
  • AI learns from data using algorithms
  • Four types: reactive, limited memory, theory of mind, self-aware
  • Benefits: service, automation, decisions. Risks: jobs, errors, ethics, cost

Week 5 — Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT)

The big ideaThe Internet of Things (IoT) connects ordinary objects to the Internet so they can collect and share data. The Industrial IoT does this in factories and farms.

The four industrial revolutions

The 1st (1760s) used steam engines and mechanisation. The 2nd (1870s) used electricity and mass production. The 3rd (1960s) brought computers and automation. The 4th, happening now, is about hyper-connectivity: AI, robots, data and IoT working together.

Example: a modern factory where machines tell each other when they need maintenance.

What IoT looks like

Sensors collect data and send it over the Internet. People or software then act on it.

Examples: smart farming sensors that track soil and livestock health; fall-detecting devices for elderly people; a smart egg tray that tells you when eggs are running out.

Advantages

IoT improves customer engagement, helps companies optimise their technology, reduces waste, and collects richer data for better decisions.

Example: a delivery company tracking every truck to reduce fuel waste.

Disadvantages

Connected devices can be hacked, so security and privacy are real worries. Systems are more complex and must obey rules and regulations.

Example: a hacker taking control of a poorly protected smart camera.

Keeping IoT safe

Devices should have to prove who they are before joining the network (authentication), and the network should enforce policies about what each device may do.

Tip: always change default passwords on any connected device.

Quick recap

  • Four industrial revolutions: steam, electricity, computers, hyper-connectivity
  • IoT = connected objects collecting data
  • Advantages: engagement, less waste, better data. Disadvantages: security, privacy, complexity

Week 7-8 — HTML Basics and CSS

The big ideaA web page is built like a house. HTML is the structure, CSS is the paint and furniture.

What HTML is

HTML means Hyper Text Markup Language. You write text and add tags to tell the browser what each part is (a heading, a paragraph, a link, an image).

Example: <h1>Welcome</h1> makes a big heading and <p>This is a paragraph.</p> makes normal text.

Structure of a page

Every page begins with <!DOCTYPE html>, which tells the browser it is an HTML5 page. Then the <html> element contains two parts: <head> (information about the page, such as the title) and <body> (what people see).

Example: a minimal page has DOCTYPE, html, head with a title, and body with a heading and paragraph.

Elements and tags

An element = start tag + content + end tag. Common ones: h1 to h6 (headings), p (paragraph), ul/ol/li (lists), a (link), img (image).

Example: <a href="https://upsa.edu.gh">Visit UPSA</a>.

Attributes

Attributes give extra information to a tag. href holds a link's address. src holds an image's file name. alt gives text describing the image.

Example: <img src="logo.png" alt="UPSA logo">.

What CSS is

CSS means Cascading Style Sheets. It controls colours, fonts, spacing and layout. A CSS rule has a selector (what to style) and a declaration block (how to style it).

Example: p { color: navy; font-size: 18px; } makes all paragraphs navy and 18 pixels.

Three ways to add CSS

Inline: inside the tag using the style attribute (quick, for one element). Internal: inside a style element in the head (for one page). External: a separate .css file linked to the page (best for whole websites).

Example: <link rel="stylesheet" href="styles.css"> in the head connects an external file.

The box model

Every element is a box. From the inside out: content, padding (space inside the border), border, and margin (space outside the border).

Example: increasing padding makes a button feel bigger inside; increasing margin pushes other items away.

div and span

div is a block container that starts on a new line, used to group sections. span is inline and sits within a line of text.

Example: wrap a whole menu in a div, and highlight one word with a span.

Quick recap

  • HTML = structure, CSS = style
  • Page: DOCTYPE, html, head, body
  • CSS rule = selector + declarations
  • Inline, internal, external CSS
  • Box model: content, padding, border, margin

Week 9-11 — JavaScript, Forms and the DOM

The big ideaJavaScript makes web pages interactive. It runs in the browser and can react when you click, type or scroll.

The web trinity

HTML builds the structure, CSS styles it, and JavaScript adds behaviour. They work together.

Example: HTML makes a button, CSS makes it blue and rounded, JavaScript shows a message when you click it.

Adding JavaScript

You can write it inline, inside a script element, or in a separate .js file linked to the page. The separate file is best for larger projects.

Example: <script src="app.js"></script> at the bottom of the page.

Variables

A variable stores a value. let holds a value that can change. const holds a value that cannot change. var is the old style and is best avoided.

Example: let score = 0; const pass = 50;

Functions

A function is a reusable block of code that runs when you call it.

Example: function add(a, b) { return a + b; } then add(2, 3) gives 5.

The DOM

The Document Object Model is the web page turned into a tree of objects that JavaScript can find and change.

Example: document.getElementById('title') finds the element with that id.

Changing the page

Select an element, then change its content or style. innerHTML sets the content. The style property changes the look.

Example: document.getElementById('msg').innerHTML = 'Hello!'; changes the text on the page.

Events

An event is something that happens, such as a click or a form submission. An event listener waits for it and then runs your code.

Example: button.addEventListener('click', function(){ alert('Clicked!'); });

Forms and validation

A form collects input using input elements, with labels to tell the user what to type. Validation checks the input before it is accepted.

Example: check that an email field is not empty and show a message if it is.

Quick recap

  • HTML structure, CSS style, JavaScript behaviour
  • let (changeable), const (fixed)
  • DOM = page as a tree; use getElementById, innerHTML
  • Events + listeners make pages interactive

Week 10 — Google Forms Add-ons and Automation

The big ideaAdd-ons and automation tools make Google Forms do more, saving you time on repetitive work.

What add-ons are

Add-ons are extra tools made by other developers that you install from the Google Workspace Marketplace to add features to Google Forms.

Example: installing an add-on to email respondents automatically.

Useful add-ons

Form Publisher creates documents or PDFs from form responses using a template, for example certificates or receipts. FormLimiter closes the form automatically after a date or a number of responses.

Example: a registration form that closes after exactly 100 responses.

Staying safe

Check what permissions an add-on asks for and give only what it needs. Free versions often have limits, such as a small number of documents per month.

Tip: avoid add-ons that ask to read all your email.

Automation platforms

Tools like Zapier and Make connect Google Forms to thousands of other apps using 'if this, then that' rules.

Example: when someone submits a form, automatically add a row to a spreadsheet and send a message in Slack.

Good practice

Automation works best when you first understand the process you want to improve, then choose the tool.

Tip: write the steps on paper before building the automation.

Quick recap

  • Add-ons extend Google Forms
  • Form Publisher and FormLimiter are examples
  • Zapier/Make connect Forms to other apps
  • Give minimal permissions
