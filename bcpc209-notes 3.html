Weeks 1-2 — What Is Law? Sources of Law in Ghana

The big ideaLaw is the set of rules that everyone in a society must follow. In Ghana, Article 11 of the 1992 Constitution tells us where the law comes from.

What law is

Law is a body of rules and regulations that control how people and organisations behave in society. It works as a tool of social control, so people behave in acceptable ways and disputes can be settled fairly.

Example: the rule that you must stop at a red light, or that you must keep your contracts.

The five sources in Article 11(1)

The laws of Ghana are: (1) the Constitution; (2) enactments made by or under the authority of Parliament; (3) orders, rules and regulations made by persons or authorities with power under the Constitution; (4) the existing law; and (5) the common law.

Tip: remember it as: Constitution, Acts, Regulations, Existing law, Common law.

The common law

Under Article 11(2), the common law includes the rules known as the common law, the doctrines of equity, and the rules of customary law.

Remember: customary law is part of Ghana's common law.

Equity

Equity developed to soften the harshness of the old common law. It gives fair remedies where strict application of the law would be unjust. Two famous maxims: 'He who comes to equity must come with clean hands' (you must be honest to ask for equity) and 'He who seeks equity must do equity' (you must be fair too).

Example: a person who cheated cannot ask the court for equitable help.

Customary law

Customary law is the unwritten rules of a particular community, developed through usage. It is recognised if it is not against natural justice or the Constitution.

Example: customary rules on land inheritance in a particular community.

The existing law

This means the written and unwritten laws of Ghana as they existed immediately before the 1992 Constitution came into force. It includes old Acts and Decrees.

Example: Acts made before 1993 that are still in force.

How a bill becomes law

A Bill must be published in the Gazette at least 14 days before it is introduced to Parliament. Bills about chieftaincy must first go to the National House of Chiefs. After Parliament passes it and the President assents, it becomes an Act.

Example: a new tax Bill is gazetted, debated and then passed.

Delegated legislation

Parliament can authorise a minister or body to make detailed rules, called delegated legislation. These are often issued as Legislative Instruments.

Example: a minister issuing regulations to apply an Act.

Cases and exam-style problems, with answers

Exam-style problem: is only an Act of Parliament law?

  1. Facts: a student says 'the only law in Ghana is what Parliament passes'
  2. Issue: what are the sources of law in Ghana?
  3. Rule: Article 11(1) lists the Constitution, enactments by or under Parliament, orders, rules and regulations under constitutional power, the existing law and the common law
  4. Application: Acts are only one source. Regulations made by a minister, laws that existed before 1993 and the common law (which includes equity and customary law) are also law
  5. Conclusion: the student is wrong; Ghana has five sources of law

Exam-style problem: can customary law be applied by the courts?

  1. Facts: a community has an unwritten custom on inheriting land. A dispute reaches the court
  2. Rule: customary law is part of the common law (Article 11(2)) but must not be against natural justice or the Constitution
  3. Application: if the custom is fair and constitutional, the court applies it; if it discriminates unfairly or breaches a constitutional right, the court refuses
  4. Conclusion: customary law is valid only to the extent it passes this test

Quick recap

  • Article 11(1): Constitution, Parliament's enactments, regulations, existing law, common law
  • Common law includes equity and customary law
  • Gazette 14 days before a Bill
  • Equity: clean hands; do equity

Weeks 1-2 — The Court System, Litigation and ADR

The big ideaGhana's courts form a ladder. Knowing which court handles what helps you know where to go when a dispute arises.

Superior courts

The Supreme Court is the final court of appeal and has exclusive power to interpret and enforce the Constitution. The Court of Appeal hears appeals only, normally with three judges. The High Court has a single judge, hears serious civil and criminal cases and has exclusive power to enforce fundamental human rights.

Example: a person whose rights were violated goes to the High Court.

Lower courts

The Circuit Court handles civil claims such as contracts and torts up to GH¢2,000,000, land and landlord-tenant cases. The District Court handles smaller claims up to GH¢500,000. The Family Court, Juvenile Court and Motor Court are District Courts.

Example: a GH¢300,000 debt claim goes to the District Court.

Regional Tribunals

These handle special criminal matters, particularly offences against the state and the public interest. They have no civil jurisdiction.

Remember: criminal only.

Problems with litigation

Going to court is slow, expensive (legal and filing fees), public and can damage relationships. Courts also face large backlogs of cases.

Example: a commercial dispute that takes years in court.

Alternative Dispute Resolution (ADR)

ADR means resolving disputes outside court. Negotiation: the parties talk and settle themselves. Mediation: a neutral person helps the parties reach their own agreement. Arbitration: a neutral third party hears both sides and decides.

Example: two business partners use a mediator instead of suing.

Why ADR is useful

It is generally faster, cheaper, more private and less hostile. It reduces court backlogs and improves access to justice. The Commercial Division of the High Court also exists to settle business disputes efficiently.

Remember: for business disputes, ADR is often the best first option.

Cases and exam-style problems, with answers

Exam-style problem: which court?

  1. Kofi sues for GH¢1,200,000 for breach of contract. Circuit Court (up to GH¢2,000,000) is the first-instance court; the District Court's limit is GH¢500,000, so it cannot hear it
  2. Ama says her fundamental human rights were violated: the High Court has exclusive power to enforce them
  3. Two parties disagree about what an article of the Constitution means: the Supreme Court has exclusive jurisdiction
  4. A person is charged with an offence against the state or public interest: Regional Tribunal (criminal only)

Exam-style problem: litigation or ADR?

  1. Two businesses have a contract dispute and want a quick, private, low-cost solution that preserves their relationship
  2. Litigation is slow, expensive and public, so they choose ADR
  3. If they want a neutral person to help them agree: mediation
  4. If they want a neutral person to decide: arbitration
  5. If they sit down and settle by themselves: negotiation

Quick recap

  • Supreme: Constitution; Appeal: appeals only; High: one judge
  • Circuit up to GH¢2m; District up to GH¢500,000
  • Negotiation, mediation, arbitration

Weeks 3-4 — Introduction to the Law of Contract and Offer

The big ideaA contract is a promise the law will enforce. It starts with one person making an offer.

What a contract is

A contract is an agreement, or set of promises, that the law will enforce. If one party breaks it, the other can go to court.

Example: you agree to buy a phone for GH¢1,500 and the seller agrees to deliver it.

The five elements

For a valid contract you need: offer, acceptance, consideration (something of value exchanged), intention to create legal relations, and capacity (legal ability to contract).

Tip: if any element is missing, there may be no enforceable contract.

Privity of contract

Only the people who are parties to a contract can enforce it or be held to it, with a few exceptions.

Example: if A promises B to pay a debt, C who is not a party cannot sue A.

What an offer is

An offer is a proposal showing that the offeror is willing to be bound if the other party accepts. In NTHC Ltd v Antwi, an offer was described as an indication, by words or conduct, that the offeror is prepared to be bound.

Example: 'I will sell you my car for GH¢20,000' is an offer.

Offer versus invitation to treat

An invitation to treat is only an invitation to negotiate. It is not an offer. In Fisher v Bell and Pharmaceutical Society v Boots, goods on display were held to be invitations to treat. The customer makes the offer at the till.

Example: items on a shop shelf are not offers; the cashier can refuse to sell.

How an offer ends

An offer can end by revocation (withdrawing it before acceptance, effective when communicated), rejection, counter-offer, lapse of time, death or insanity of a party, or destruction of the subject matter.

Example: if the seller tells you before you accept that the offer is cancelled, you can no longer accept.

Counter-offer

A counter-offer is a reply that changes the terms. It acts as a rejection of the original offer, so the original offer disappears.

Example: offer 'GH¢20,000'; reply 'I will pay GH¢15,000.' The first offer is dead.

Cases and exam-style problems, with answers

Case: Fisher v Bell (1961)

  1. Facts: a shopkeeper displayed a flick knife in his shop window with a price tag, and was prosecuted for 'offering for sale' an offensive weapon
  2. Decision: displaying goods is only an invitation to treat, not an offer, so he was not guilty
  3. Why it matters: the customer, not the shop, makes the offer when asking to buy

Case: Pharmaceutical Society v Boots (1953)

  1. Facts: in a self-service pharmacy, customers took goods from shelves and paid at the till
  2. Decision: the goods on shelves were an invitation to treat. The customer makes the offer at the till, and the cashier accepts
  3. Why it matters: the shop can refuse to sell at the till

Exam-style problem: the wrongly priced phone

  1. Facts: a shop displays a phone marked GH¢150 by mistake instead of GH¢1,500. A customer takes it to the till and demands to buy it at GH¢150
  2. Rule: display of goods is an invitation to treat (Fisher v Bell; Boots)
  3. Application: the customer's request at the till is the offer; the shop is free to accept or refuse
  4. Conclusion: the shop does not have to sell at GH¢150

Exam-style problem: counter-offer

  1. Facts: Kwame offers to sell his car for GH¢20,000. Esi replies, 'I will pay GH¢15,000.' The next day she says, 'OK, I will pay GH¢20,000'
  2. Rule: a counter-offer rejects the original offer
  3. Application: Esi's first reply killed Kwame's original offer. Her later 'OK' is a new offer that Kwame may accept or refuse
  4. Conclusion: there is no contract until Kwame accepts

Quick recap

  • Contract = enforceable agreement
  • Five elements: offer, acceptance, consideration, intention, capacity
  • Display of goods = invitation to treat
  • Counter-offer rejects the offer

Week 4 — Acceptance and Consideration

The big ideaOnce there is an offer, a contract needs acceptance and consideration.

What acceptance is

Acceptance is a final, unqualified agreement to the terms of an offer. It can be express (in words) or implied from conduct. When it happens, the offer becomes a contract.

Example: 'I accept your offer' or paying the price.

Acceptance must be absolute

If the reply adds conditions or changes terms, it is a counter-offer, not an acceptance.

Example: 'I accept but I want delivery free' is not acceptance.

The method of acceptance

If the offeror says how to accept, the offeree should use that method or another that is just as quick. The case Manchester Diocesan Council for Education v Commercial and General Investments is cited.

Example: if the offer says 'reply by email', replying by email is correct.

Knowing about the offer

The person accepting must know about the offer when they accept.

Example: if someone finds your lost phone without knowing about your reward, they cannot claim it under the offer.

What consideration is

Consideration is the price, inducement or reason for a promise. It can be a benefit to the promisor or a detriment to the promisee. It does not have to be money.

Example: in a tenancy, rent paid is consideration for the right to occupy the flat.

When consideration fails

If the consideration given completely fails, there is no valid contract.

Example: you pay for a car that never existed.

Cases and exam-style problems, with answers

Exam-style problem: conditional acceptance

  1. Facts: Ama is offered 50 bags of cement at GH¢100 each. She replies, 'I accept, but delivery must be free'
  2. Rule: acceptance must be absolute and unqualified
  3. Application: adding a condition changes the terms, so it is a counter-offer
  4. Conclusion: no contract yet

Exam-style problem: a promise without consideration

  1. Facts: Mr Mensah promises to give his neighbour GH¢500 next month. The neighbour gives nothing in return
  2. Rule: a contract needs consideration, a benefit to the promisor or a detriment to the promisee
  3. Application: the neighbour gave nothing, so this is a gift promise
  4. Conclusion: the promise is not enforceable as a contract

Quick recap

  • Acceptance: final, unqualified; turns offer into contract
  • Method of acceptance prescribed by offeror
  • Consideration = price or inducement; benefit or detriment

Week 5 — Intention to Create Legal Relations and Capacity

The big ideaEven with an offer, acceptance and consideration, a contract needs both parties to intend legal consequences and to have the legal ability to contract.

Intention to create legal relations

The parties must intend their agreement to be enforceable by law. Social and domestic promises are usually just that: promises between family or friends.

Example: 'I will cook dinner for you tomorrow' is not a contract.

Commercial agreements

In business dealings, the courts presume the parties intended legal relations. This can be rebutted if the agreement clearly says it is not binding.

Example: a supply agreement between two firms is presumed binding.

Domestic agreements

Agreements between husband and wife or parent and child in a domestic setting are presumed not to create legal relations.

Example: a promise by a father to give pocket money.

Capacity of minors

In general, a contract between a minor and an adult is not binding on the minor but binds the adult. The lecture notes the contractual age in Ghana is 21, although other laws, such as the Children's Act 1998, set the age of majority at 18 for other purposes.

Example: a minor who buys a car on credit can usually avoid the contract.

Necessaries

Contracts for necessaries bind a minor. Under the Sale of Goods Act, 1962 (Act 137), s.2(3), necessaries are goods suitable to the minor's condition in life and actual requirements at the time of delivery.

Example: food and clothing for a minor.

Beneficial contracts of service

Contracts that benefit the minor, such as apprenticeships, are binding.

Example: a 16-year-old learning a trade under an apprenticeship.

Voidable contracts and trade

Some contracts are voidable: binding until the minor chooses to avoid them. A minor trader is generally not liable for goods supplied for trade.

Remember: the law protects minors from exploitation.

Companies

A company has the powers of a natural person of full capacity.

Example: a company can sign contracts through its directors.

Cases and exam-style problems, with answers

Exam-style problem: the 16-year-old and the laptop

  1. Facts: a 16-year-old buys a laptop on credit for GH¢3,000 to start tutoring
  2. Rule: a contract with a minor is not binding on the minor but binds the adult; exceptions are necessaries and beneficial contracts of service (Act 137, s.2(3))
  3. Application: a laptop for a business is unlikely to be a necessary suited to his condition in life and actual requirements
  4. Conclusion: the minor is probably not bound, though the seller is

Exam-style problem: the family promise

  1. Facts: a husband promises his wife a monthly allowance of GH¢500
  2. Rule: agreements between husband and wife in a domestic setting are presumed not to create legal relations
  3. Application: nothing shows they meant it to be legally enforceable
  4. Conclusion: no enforceable contract

Exam-style problem: supply agreement

  1. Facts: two companies sign an agreement to supply and pay for goods
  2. Rule: commercial agreements are presumed to create legal relations
  3. Conclusion: it is a binding contract unless it clearly says it is not

Quick recap

  • Commercial presumed binding; domestic presumed not
  • Minors: not bound unless necessaries or beneficial service contracts
  • Act 137 s.2(3) defines necessaries

Week 6 — Mistake and Misrepresentation

The big ideaSometimes consent to a contract is flawed. These flaws are called vitiating factors, and they can affect the contract's validity.

What mistake is

A mistake is when a party contracts on terms, with a person or about a subject matter they did not intend. Mistakes can be unilateral (one party) or common (both parties make the same mistake).

Example: you agree to buy a plot thinking it is in Accra when it is not.

Unilateral mistake

Generally, a unilateral mistake does not affect the contract's validity because the other party did nothing wrong.

Remember: read before you sign.

Common mistake

If both parties are mistaken about a fundamental fact, the contract may be void. In Bell v Lever Brothers, Lord Atkin explained that if the subject matter has already ceased to exist, the contract is void.

Example: both parties think a ship exists; it actually sank last week.

Mistake about quality

In Magee v Pennine Insurance, the court held that a mistake only about quality generally does not make the contract void.

Example: buying a painting believing it is by a famous painter when it is not.

What misrepresentation is

A misrepresentation is a false statement of fact made before the contract that induces the other party to contract. It is not part of the contract terms.

Example: a seller says a car has never been in an accident when it has.

Three types

Fraudulent: made knowingly, without belief in its truth, or recklessly. Negligent: made carelessly. Innocent: made honestly, believing it true.

Example: an honest mistake by a seller about a car's mileage is innocent.

Remedies

A contract induced by fraudulent misrepresentation is voidable at the option of the person misled (Clarke v Dickson). For innocent misrepresentation, the person misled may rescind (cancel the contract).

Remember: voidable means valid until the innocent party chooses to cancel.

Cases and exam-style problems, with answers

Case: Bell v Lever Brothers (1932)

  1. Facts: Lever Brothers paid two managers, Bell and Snelling, large sums as compensation for loss of office. Later it was discovered that they had committed breaches that would have allowed dismissal without compensation. Lever Brothers asked for the money back
  2. Decision: the House of Lords held the mistake was not fundamental, so the compensation agreements stood
  3. Why it matters: Lord Atkin explained when a common mistake makes a contract void, such as where the subject matter does not exist. A mistake about quality rarely will

Exam-style problem: the used car

  1. Facts: a seller tells a buyer the car has never been in an accident, although he knows it has. The buyer pays GH¢40,000
  2. Rule: a false statement of fact that induces a contract is a misrepresentation; fraudulent misrepresentation makes the contract voidable
  3. Application: the statement was knowingly false, so it is fraudulent
  4. Conclusion: the buyer can rescind (cancel) the contract and recover his money (Clarke v Dickson principle)

Quick recap

  • Unilateral mistake: usually no effect
  • Common mistake about existence: void
  • Misrepresentation: false statement of fact that induces contract
  • Fraudulent, negligent, innocent

Week 7 — Duress, Undue Influence and Illegality

The big ideaA contract must be freely agreed and lawful. Pressure, unfair influence and illegality can stop a contract being enforced.

Duress

Duress is obtaining someone's agreement by improper pressure or threats. The contract is voidable by the victim.

Example: threatening someone to make them sign over land.

Undue influence

Undue influence is when one person uses their influence over another to get the other to agree to a contract that disadvantages them. Courts may presume undue influence where there is a relationship of trust and confidence and the transaction is unfair.

Example: an elderly person signing property over to a caregiver.

Illegal contracts

A contract whose purpose, subject matter or performance breaks the law will not be enforced.

Example: a contract to sell prohibited drugs.

Contracts against public policy

Contracts that harm the public interest are not enforced.

Example: an agreement to bribe an official.

Restraint of trade

A clause that stops someone from working in their trade is enforceable only if it is reasonable and just between the parties and in the public interest.

Example: a clause stopping an ex-employee from working within 5 km for 6 months may be reasonable; one stopping them for life across the country is not.

Void and voidable

A void contract has no legal effect from the start. A voidable contract is valid until the innocent party sets it aside.

Remember: duress and undue influence make a contract voidable; illegality often makes it void.

Cases and exam-style problems, with answers

Exam-style problem: pressure to sign

  1. Facts: a landlord threatens to evict a tenant and harm his family unless he signs a new lease with much higher rent
  2. Rule: consent obtained by improper pressure is duress and the contract is voidable
  3. Conclusion: the tenant can set the lease aside

Exam-style problem: the restraint of trade clause

  1. Facts: an employee's contract forbids him from working for any competitor anywhere in Ghana for life
  2. Rule: a restraint of trade is enforceable only if it is reasonable and just between the parties and in the public interest
  3. Application: a lifetime ban nationwide goes beyond protecting the employer's legitimate interests
  4. Conclusion: the clause is unenforceable; a shorter, local restriction might be upheld

Exam-style problem: the smuggling agreement

  1. Facts: two traders agree to smuggle goods to avoid duty
  2. Rule: contracts whose purpose breaks the law are illegal and not enforced
  3. Conclusion: neither party can sue on the agreement

Quick recap

  • Duress = improper pressure; voidable
  • Undue influence = abuse of trust
  • Illegal and against public policy: not enforced
  • Restraint of trade must be reasonable

Week 8 — Discharge of Contracts

The big ideaDischarge means the contract has ended and the parties no longer have to perform. There are several ways this can happen.

Discharge by performance

Normally, a party must perform completely and exactly. If they fall short, they cannot demand payment.

Example: a painter paints only 3 of 4 rooms and cannot claim the full price.

Exceptions to exact performance

Substantial performance: if the shortfall is small, the contractor is paid less a deduction. Divisible contracts: each part is a separate contract. Acceptance of part performance: if you accept it, you pay for it. Prevention: if the other party stops you from performing, you are excused.

Example: a builder finishes the house with minor defects and gets paid minus the cost of fixing them.

Discharge by agreement

The parties may agree to end or replace the contract.

Example: two parties sign a new contract that cancels the old one.

Frustration

A contract is frustrated when, after it is made, events beyond the parties' control make performance impossible or radically different. In Taylor v Caldwell, a music hall hired for concerts was destroyed by fire before the dates, and the contract was discharged.

Example: a contract to hire a venue that burns down.

Breach of contract

A breach occurs when a party fails to perform without a lawful excuse. Every breach lets the innocent party claim damages. A serious (fundamental) breach also lets them end the contract.

Example: a supplier delivers the wrong goods.

Anticipatory breach

A party announces before the performance date that they will not perform. The innocent party can sue immediately or wait. Frost v Knight is the leading case.

Example: a seller says in March that he will not deliver the goods due in June.

Cases and exam-style problems, with answers

Case: Taylor v Caldwell (1863)

  1. Facts: Caldwell agreed to hire Surrey Gardens Music Hall to Taylor for a series of concerts. Before the first concert, a fire destroyed the hall
  2. Decision: the contract was discharged by frustration, because the subject matter was destroyed without anyone's fault. Neither party could sue the other for breach
  3. Why it matters: it created the doctrine of frustration

Case: Frost v Knight (1872)

  1. Facts: Knight promised to marry Frost on his father's death. While his father was still alive he refused to marry her
  2. Decision: Frost could sue immediately for breach, without waiting for the father's death
  3. Why it matters: it is the leading case on anticipatory breach

Exam-style problem: the unfinished building

  1. Facts: a builder agreed to build a house for GH¢200,000. He finishes everything but leaves a few minor defects costing GH¢3,000 to fix
  2. Rule: performance must normally be complete and exact, but substantial performance is an exception
  3. Application: the defects are minor
  4. Conclusion: the builder can claim GH¢200,000 less the GH¢3,000 cost of repair

Quick recap

  • Performance must be complete and exact, with exceptions
  • Frustration: Taylor v Caldwell
  • Breach always gives damages
  • Anticipatory breach: Frost v Knight

Weeks 9-10 — Agency

The big ideaAgency lets one person act for another so that the second person is bound by the first person's actions.

What agency is

An agent is appointed by a principal to represent the principal and make contracts with third parties on the principal's behalf. The principal is bound by those contracts. It is a fiduciary relationship, meaning it is based on trust.

Example: an estate agent selling your house for you.

How agency is created

By agreement (express, in words, or implied from conduct), by implication, or by necessity. There is generally no formality required, except that an agent must be appointed by deed to sign a deed.

Example: a shop owner leaving an employee in charge of the shop.

Agency of necessity

An agency can arise in an emergency when it is impossible to get the principal's instructions. The agent must control the principal's property, face a real emergency, and act in good faith in everyone's interest.

Example: a carrier selling perishable fruit that will rot because the owner cannot be reached.

Authority of an agent

Express authority is what the principal actually gave. Implied authority covers what is reasonable, incidental and necessary to carry out the job (Ryan v Pilkington).

Example: an agent hired to sell a house may advertise it, even if not told so.

Duties of an agent

The agent must be loyal (fiduciary duty): no secret profits, no conflicts of interest. The agent must use reasonable skill and care and follow the principal's lawful instructions.

Example: an agent who accepts a secret commission from the buyer breaches this duty.

Duties of the principal

The principal must pay the agent's agreed commission and indemnify (compensate) the agent for liabilities properly incurred in carrying out the agency.

Example: reimbursing the agent's reasonable expenses.

Cases and exam-style problems, with answers

Case: Springer v Great Western Railway (1921)

  1. Facts: tomatoes were being carried to market, but the journey was badly delayed and the railway company sold them without asking the owner for instructions
  2. Decision: the court held there was no agency of necessity, because the railway could have contacted the owner
  3. Why it matters: agency of necessity requires that it is truly impossible to get the principal's instructions, as well as a real emergency and good faith

Exam-style problem: the agent who exceeds authority

  1. Facts: Esi appoints Yaw to sell her house for at least GH¢500,000. Yaw sells it for GH¢300,000
  2. Rule: an agent must follow instructions and has only express and implied authority
  3. Application: selling below the stated minimum is outside Yaw's authority and breaches his duty
  4. Conclusion: Yaw is in breach; Esi is not bound by the unauthorised sale unless she approves it

Exam-style problem: the secret commission

  1. Facts: an agent buying machinery for a company secretly accepts GH¢5,000 from the seller
  2. Rule: an agent owes a fiduciary duty and must not make secret profits
  3. Conclusion: the agent is in breach and the principal can claim the secret profit

Quick recap

  • Agent acts for principal
  • Created by agreement, implication or necessity
  • Express vs implied authority
  • Agent: fiduciary duty, skill, care, obey instructions; principal indemnifies agent

Weeks 11-12 — Sale of Goods

The big ideaThe Sale of Goods Act, 1962 (Act 137) governs contracts where goods are sold for money. It sets out when ownership passes and what each side must do.

What a sale of goods is

Under s.1(1), it is a contract where the seller agrees to transfer the property in goods to the buyer for a price which is wholly or partly money. Barter is goods exchanged for goods. A gift has no price. Bailment gives temporary possession but not ownership.

Example: you pay GH¢500 for a TV; ownership passes to you.

Kinds of goods

Specific goods are identified and agreed upon when the contract is made. Unascertained goods are not yet identified, described only by type. Future goods do not yet exist.

Example: 'this particular fridge' (specific) versus '50 bags of cement' (unascertained).

Who owns the goods? The nemo dat rule

Under s.28, nobody can give a better title than they have. A thief cannot sell good title. There are exceptions, for example a buyer who takes in good faith from someone who has possession with the owner's permission.

Example: if you buy a stolen phone, the true owner can take it back.

Sale or return

The buyer can try the goods. Ownership passes when the buyer approves, adopts the goods or keeps them beyond the agreed time without returning.

Example: a shop takes goods from a supplier on sale-or-return terms.

The seller's duties

The seller must have the right to sell (s.10), deliver the right quantity (s.14), deliver at the right time, supply goods that match a sample or description (s.8(2)), and of satisfactory quality and fit for purpose (s.13).

Example: a TV that fails after a day breaches the quality duty.

The buyer's duties

Under ss.21-22 the buyer must accept delivery and pay the price as agreed.

Example: refusing to take goods that match the contract is a breach.

Cases and exam-style problems, with answers

Exam-style problem: the stolen phone

  1. Facts: a thief sells a stolen phone to Kojo, who pays in good faith
  2. Rule: s.28 of Act 137: a non-owner cannot pass a better title than he has
  3. Application: the thief had no title, so Kojo gets none
  4. Conclusion: the true owner can recover the phone; Kojo's remedy is against the thief

Exam-style problem: the faulty TV

  1. Facts: a buyer purchases a TV and it fails after one day
  2. Rule: the seller must supply goods of satisfactory quality and fit for purpose (s.13)
  3. Application: the defect existed at delivery and was not shown on a reasonable examination
  4. Conclusion: the seller is in breach; the buyer may reject the goods or claim damages

Exam-style problem: sale or return

  1. Facts: a shop takes 20 shirts from a supplier on sale or return and keeps them for months without returning or paying
  2. Rule: ownership passes to the buyer when he approves, adopts the goods, or keeps them beyond the agreed time
  3. Conclusion: the shop is deemed to have accepted the shirts and must pay

Quick recap

  • s.1(1): goods for a price
  • Specific, unascertained, future goods
