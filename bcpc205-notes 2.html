Week 1 — Overview of Marketing 1

The big ideaMarketing is everything a business does to find out what customers need and then give it to them in a way that also earns the business a profit.

What marketing really is

Many people think marketing means advertising or selling. In fact, selling is only the last step. The American Marketing Association says marketing is the activity, set of institutions and processes for creating, communicating, delivering and exchanging offerings that have value for customers, clients, partners and society. The UK's CIM says it is the management process responsible for identifying, anticipating and satisfying customer requirements profitably.

Example: before a company launches a new drink, it asks consumers what taste they want, designs the product, sets a fair price, makes it available in shops and then promotes it. All of this is marketing.

Identify, anticipate, satisfy

Identifying means finding out what customers want now (through market research). Anticipating means predicting what they will want next (through strategic planning). Satisfying means delivering it through the product, price, promotion, distribution and personal selling.

Example: a phone company notices students want cheaper data bundles (identify), expects more video streaming next year (anticipate), and launches a night bundle (satisfy).

Common wrong ideas

Marketing is not just advertising, not just selling, and not tricking people into buying what they do not need. It is a concept, a business function, an ongoing process and a system.

Remember: a firm that only sells what it already makes, without asking customers, is not doing marketing.

The functions of marketing

The functions are distribution, financing, marketing information management, product and service management, promotion and selling.

Example: a shop owner studying which items customers ask for is doing marketing information management.

Needs, wants and demands

A need is a state of feeling deprived, such as hunger. A want is how a need takes shape given your culture and personality, such as wanting fufu or rice. A demand is a want backed by buying power: you want it and can pay for it.

Example: many people want a car (want), but only those with money demand one.

Marketing myopia

Marketing myopia is focusing on the product and forgetting the underlying need. A firm that thinks 'we sell cassette players' misses that customers wanted music.

Remember: always ask what need your product satisfies.

Exchange and markets

Exchange is obtaining something you want by giving something in return. A market is the set of actual and potential buyers of a product.

Example: you give GH¢5 and receive a sachet of water.

Quick recap

  • Marketing is more than advertising and selling
  • Identify, anticipate, satisfy customers profitably
  • Need, want, demand
  • Myopia = focusing on the product, not the need

Week 2 — Overview of Marketing 2

The big ideaFirms have different attitudes towards marketing. Over time, thinking moved from 'what can we make?' to 'what do customers and society need?'.

Value proposition

A value proposition is the set of benefits a company promises to deliver to meet customers' needs. It explains why a customer should choose you.

Example: 'Fast delivery in 30 minutes' is the promise of a food delivery app.

Production concept

Consumers will prefer products that are widely available and cheap, so the firm focuses on production efficiency and distribution.

Example: a firm making basic plastic chairs in huge quantities to sell cheaply.

Product concept

Consumers prefer products with the best quality, performance and features, so the firm keeps improving the product.

Risk: the firm may build a 'better mousetrap' that nobody wants.

Selling concept

Consumers will not buy enough unless the firm uses heavy selling and promotion. It pushes what it has made.

Example: aggressive door-to-door selling of insurance.

Marketing concept

The firm succeeds by understanding target customers' needs and delivering satisfaction better than competitors. It starts with the customer.

Example: a restaurant redesigning its menu after surveying its customers.

Societal marketing concept

The firm also considers society and the long-term interests of consumers, not only their immediate wants and the firm's profit.

Example: a drinks company using recyclable bottles.

The marketing mix: the 4 Ps

Product (what you sell), Price (what it costs), Promotion (how you communicate) and Place (where and how it is available). Together they form the integrated marketing program.

Example: for a sachet water brand: product is the water, price GH¢1, promotion is adverts, place is shops and street sellers.

Relationships

Partner relationship management means working closely with partners inside the company and outside (suppliers, distributors) to bring more value to customers. Customer relationship management is building long-term profitable relationships by delivering superior value and satisfaction.

Example: a supermarket giving loyalty cards.

Quick recap

  • Five concepts: production, product, selling, marketing, societal
  • 4 Ps: product, price, promotion, place
  • CRM builds long-term relationships

Week 3 — The Marketing Environment

The big ideaNo business operates alone. The surroundings it works in, called the marketing environment, create both opportunities and threats.

What the marketing environment is

It consists of the actors and forces outside marketing that affect management's ability to build and keep successful relationships with target customers. It has two layers: a close layer (micro-environment) and a remote layer (macro-environment).

Example: a new law banning plastic bags affects every shop owner.

The micro-environment

These are forces close to the firm: the company's own departments, customers, suppliers, marketing intermediaries (resellers, distribution firms, marketing agencies, banks), competitors and publics.

Example: if your flour supplier raises prices, your bakery's costs rise.

Publics

A public is any group with an actual or potential interest in the firm, such as media, government, local community, citizens' groups and the firm's own employees.

Example: a factory polluting a river will face anger from the local community.

The macro-environment

These are broad forces that the firm cannot control: demographic (population, age, gender), economic (income, inflation, interest rates), natural (resources, weather, pollution), technological, political and legal, and socio-cultural.

Example: rising inflation reduces what customers can afford.

Types of customer markets

Consumer markets (individuals), business markets, reseller markets, institutional markets (schools, hospitals), government markets and international markets.

Example: a school buying bulk stationery is an institutional customer.

Economic and demographic forces

The economic environment affects purchasing power and spending patterns. The demographic environment is about people: size, density, location, age, gender and occupation. People make up markets.

Example: Ghana's young population creates demand for mobile data, fashion and education.

Responding to the environment

Firms either adapt to changes or try to influence the environment (environmental management). Changes in one force can affect others.

Example: when mobile money rules changed, telecom companies adapted their services.

Quick recap

  • Micro: company, customers, suppliers, intermediaries, competitors, publics
  • Macro: demographic, economic, natural, technological, political/legal, socio-cultural
  • Firms must adapt or influence

Week 5 — Understanding Consumer Behaviour

The big ideaConsumer behaviour is the study of how and why people buy. If you understand it, you can design products and messages that fit.

What it is

It is the buying behaviour of final consumers: individuals and households who buy goods and services for personal use, not for resale.

Example: you buying a phone for yourself.

Types of buying behaviour

Routine response: frequent, low-value, little thought. Limited decision making: some thought. Extensive decision making: high-value or rare purchases with much research. Impulse buying: a sudden strong urge to buy immediately.

Examples: buying bread (routine), a laptop (extensive), a snack at the till (impulse).

The decision process

1. Problem recognition (you notice a gap between where you are and where you want to be). 2. Information search. 3. Evaluation of alternatives. 4. Purchase decision. 5. Post-purchase evaluation.

Example: your phone breaks, you ask friends and read reviews, compare three models, buy one, then decide whether you are happy.

Evoked set, brand image and risk

The evoked set is the few brands a consumer actually considers. Brand image is the set of beliefs a consumer holds about a brand. Perceived risk, the worry that a purchase may go wrong, slows decisions.

Example: you only compare three phone brands out of many in the market.

Cognitive dissonance

After buying, you may feel uneasy or doubt your choice. Marketers reduce this with warranties, follow-up calls and reassurance.

Example: a 'thank you' message and a guarantee after a big purchase.

Factors that influence buying

Personal: demographics, situation and involvement. Psychological: motivation, perception and personality. Social: culture and sub-culture, roles and status, reference groups, family and social class.

Example: a student buys a brand because friends use it (reference group).

Buying roles

In a purchase, people may play different roles: initiator (suggests it), influencer, decider, buyer and user.

Example: a child asks for a toy (initiator), a parent decides and pays (decider and buyer), the child uses it (user).

Quick recap

  • Five stages: problem, search, evaluation, purchase, post-purchase
  • Personal, psychological, social factors
  • Roles: initiator, influencer, decider, buyer, user

Week 6 — Segmentation, Targeting and Positioning

The big ideaYou cannot serve everyone equally well. Segmentation, targeting and positioning (STP) is how a firm chooses its customers and how it wants to be seen.

Segmentation

Segmentation divides a large, varied market into smaller groups with similar needs, which the firm can reach efficiently.

Example: instead of selling 'clothes to everyone', a firm focuses on 'school uniforms for primary pupils'.

Four bases for segmenting

Geographic (region, city, climate). Demographic (age, gender, income, occupation). Psychographic (lifestyle, social class, personality). Behavioural (occasion, benefits sought, usage rate, loyalty).

Example: a bank offering student accounts is using demographic segmentation.

A good segment

It must be measurable, accessible, profitable (large enough), differentiable and actionable.

Test: can you find them, reach them, and make money from them?

Choosing target markets

Undifferentiated: one offer for the whole market. Differentiated: separate offers for several segments. Concentrated (niche): focus on one or a few segments, ideal for small firms. Micromarketing: tailoring to individuals or very local groups.

Example: a small boutique that only sells wedding fabrics is niche marketing.

Positioning

Positioning is the place your product holds in customers' minds compared with competitors. You can position on attributes, price and quality, user, or use.

Example: a brand positioned as 'the affordable choice' versus 'the premium choice'.

Repositioning

Firms reposition when customers' needs change, new competitors appear or technology changes.

Example: a traditional newspaper repositioning as a digital news brand.

Quick recap

  • Segment, target, position
  • Bases: geographic, demographic, psychographic, behavioural
  • Strategies: undifferentiated, differentiated, concentrated, micromarketing
  • Positioning = place in customers' minds

Week 8 — Setting Product Strategy

The big ideaThe product is the heart of marketing. Everything else (price, promotion, place) exists to support it.

What a product is

A product is anything offered to a market for attention, acquisition, use or consumption that might satisfy a want or need. It can be a good, a service, an idea or an experience.

Example: a haircut is a service product.

Consumer goods

Durable goods last a long time (fridge). Non-durable goods are used up fast (bread). By buying habit: convenience products (low-value, everyday), shopping products (compared before buying), specialty products (unique, buyers make special efforts) and unsought products (not thought about until needed).

Example: sachet water (convenience), a TV (shopping), a luxury watch (specialty), life insurance (unsought).

Product life cycle

Introduction: sales low, profits little or none, heavy promotion. Growth: sales climb quickly and profits rise. Maturity: sales growth slows, profits stabilise, competition is strong. Decline: sales and profits fall.

Example: feature phones are in decline, while smartphones are in maturity.

Extending the life cycle

A firm can modify the product, change its distribution, adjust its price or run new promotions.

Example: a soft drink launching a new flavour to revive sales.

The BCG matrix

It classifies products by market growth rate and relative market share. Stars: high growth, high share (invest). Question marks: high growth, low share (decide). Cash cows: low growth, high share (milk for cash). Dogs: low growth, low share (consider dropping).

Example: a mature biscuit with a dominant share is a cash cow.

New product development

Steps: idea generation, idea screening, business analysis, product development, test marketing, commercialisation (full launch).

Example: a company tests a new snack in one region (test marketing) before launching nationally.

Why new products fail

Common reasons: overestimating the market, poor design, wrong timing, wrong price and weak promotion.

Remember: test marketing reduces the risk.

Quick recap

  • Convenience, shopping, specialty, unsought
  • PLC: introduction, growth, maturity, decline
  • BCG: stars, question marks, cash cows, dogs
  • NPD has six stages

Week 9 — Pricing Strategies and Programmes

The big ideaPrice is the only part of the marketing mix that brings in money. The others cost money.

What price is

Price is the amount of money charged for a product or service, or the sum of values customers exchange for its benefits.

Example: GH¢2 for a loaf of bread.

Internal and external influences

Internal: the firm's marketing objectives, cost pattern and other products. External: competitors, elasticity of demand (how much demand changes when price changes), suppliers, customers and government policy.

Example: if competitors cut prices, you may have to follow.

Pricing objectives

Income-oriented: target return or profit maximisation. Sales-oriented: growing sales or market share. Status-quo: meeting competition or avoiding price wars (non-price competition).

Example: a new network pricing low to gain market share.

Skimming

Charge a high price at launch to earn high returns from customers willing to pay, then lower it later.

Example: a new smartphone priced high at launch.

Penetration

Charge one low price to the whole market to win a large share quickly. It works when demand is elastic.

Example: a new soap sold cheaply to gain customers fast.

Other pricing tactics

Destroyer pricing means cutting prices to hurt competitors or scare off new entrants. Promotional pricing uses loss leaders: items sold at little or no profit to draw customers in.

Example: a supermarket selling sugar at cost to attract shoppers who then buy other items.

Price or non-price competition

Price competition suits firms with low costs that can respond quickly. Non-price competition focuses on quality, brand and service instead.

Example: a premium brand avoids price wars and stresses quality.

Quick recap

  • Price brings in revenue
  • Skimming = high first; penetration = low first
  • Loss leader attracts customers
  • Objectives: income, sales, status quo

Week 10 — Place and Marketing Channels

The big ideaPlace (distribution) is about getting the product to the right place, in the right quantity, at the right time.

Marketing channels

A channel is a group of interdependent organisations that help make a product available. Producer to consumer is a direct channel. Using intermediaries makes it an indirect channel.

Example: a farmer selling to a market woman, who sells to households.

Why use intermediaries

They reduce the number of contacts and transactions, lower storage costs, sort and bundle goods into useful assortments, and bring expertise.

Example: instead of 100 farmers each visiting 100 shops, one wholesaler connects them.

Types of intermediary

Wholesalers buy in bulk from producers and sell to retailers. Agents and brokers bring buyers and sellers together. Retailers sell small quantities to final consumers (supermarkets, department stores, discount stores, mail order). A franchise is permission to sell a product in a specified area.

Example: a franchise restaurant operating under a famous brand name.

Choosing a channel

Judge channels by economic performance (sales and cost), control (how much you control how it is sold) and adaptability (flexibility to change).

Example: selling through your own shop gives more control but costs more.

Distribution intensity

Intensive: as many outlets as possible (soft drinks). Selective: a few chosen outlets (electronics). Exclusive: one outlet in an area (luxury cars).

Example: a luxury brand uses exclusive distribution to protect its image.

Vertical marketing systems

Corporate: one owner controls all stages. Administered: the largest member coordinates the others. Contractual: members are linked by formal agreements, such as franchises.

Example: a manufacturer that also owns its shops is a corporate VMS.

Physical distribution

This is the transport and storage of goods. Transport modes: road, rail, air, sea, inland waterways, pipelines. Factors: cost, time, accessibility, reliability, traceability and capability.

Example: perishable goods go by fast transport.

Quick recap

  • Channel = organisations that make a product available
  • Intermediaries save contacts and storage
  • Intensive, selective, exclusive
  • VMS: corporate, administered, contractual

Week 11 — Promotion

The big ideaPromotion is how a firm talks to its market. The aim is to inform, persuade and remind.

Goals of promotion

Inform customers that a product exists, persuade them to prefer it, and remind them to keep buying.

Example: a new product launch (inform), a comparison advert (persuade), a familiar jingle (remind).

The communication process

A sender encodes a message and sends it through a channel. The receiver decodes it and gives feedback. Noise (distractions or misunderstandings) can disrupt the process.

Example: a radio advert drowned out by traffic noise fails to communicate.

The promotional mix

Four tools: advertising, personal selling, sales promotion and public relations.

Remember: firms usually combine them.

Advertising

Advertising is a paid, non-personal presentation of ideas, goods or services through mass media by an identified sponsor. The steps of a campaign: identify the target market, set objectives, design the message, set a budget, choose media, execute and evaluate.

Example: a TV advert for a bank.

Personal selling

This is persuasive two-way communication between seller and buyer. Steps: preparation, prospecting (finding leads), pre-approach, presentation, handling objections, closing the sale and post-sale follow-up.

Example: an insurance agent meeting a client and answering their doubts before asking for the signature.

Sales promotion

Short-term incentives that add value. Consumer-oriented (discounts, coupons, contests), trade-oriented (free goods to retailers) and salesforce-oriented (bonuses and commissions).

Example: 'buy two, get one free'.

Public relations

PR builds good relations with the public through favourable publicity and a strong corporate image. Tools include press relations, product publicity, corporate communication, lobbying and counselling.

Example: a company sponsoring a community clean-up and getting news coverage.

Quick recap

  • Inform, persuade, remind
  • Mix: advertising, personal selling, sales promotion, PR
  • Selling steps: prepare, prospect, pre-approach, present, objections, close, follow-up

Week 12 — Building and Maintaining Customer Relationships

The big ideaIt costs far more to find a new customer than to keep an existing one, so smart firms build long-term relationships.

What CRM is

Customer relationship management is the overall process of building and keeping profitable customer relationships by delivering superior value and satisfaction.

Example: a bank remembering your preferences and offering better service over time.

Value and satisfaction

Customer-perceived value is the difference between total customer value (benefits) and total customer cost. Satisfaction depends on how the product's performance compares with what the customer expected.

Example: delivery that arrives earlier than promised delights the customer.

Relationship levels

Selective relationship management means focusing on fewer, more profitable customers. Customer-managed relationships are those where customers, using digital tools, interact with the company and each other (reviews, social media).

Example: customers comparing your product in online reviews.

Partners and the supply chain

Partner relationship management links you with suppliers and distributors. A supply chain is the path from raw materials to components to final products to final buyers.

Example: flour mill to bakery to shop to consumer.

Customer lifetime value

This is the value of the entire stream of purchases a customer makes over a lifetime of loyalty.

Example: a customer spending GH¢50 a month for 10 years is worth GH¢6,000, not just GH¢50.

Share of customer and customer equity

Share of customer is the portion of a customer's spending in a category that the firm gets. Customer equity is the total of the lifetime values of all the firm's customers.

Remember: the higher the customer equity, the more valuable the firm.

Building the capability

Firms need data collection, marketing tools, customisation and customer service procedures that make customers feel known and valued.

Example: a loyalty card that records purchases to offer personal rewards.

Quick recap

  • CRM = long-term profitable relationships
  • Perceived value = benefits - costs
  • CLV, share of customer, customer equity
