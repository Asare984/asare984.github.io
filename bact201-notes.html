Week 1 — Introduction to Accounting

Accounting is the language of business. It records, summarises and communicates financial information to users.

Key points

  • Modern accounting owes its origin to Luca Pacioli, who published his work in 1494.
  • Definition: identifying, measuring and communicating economic information to permit informed judgements and decisions.
  • Aims: keep systematic records, protect property, find profit or loss, show financial position, support decisions.
  • Financial statements: statement of financial position, statement of comprehensive income, statement of changes in equity, cash flow statement and notes.
  • Standard setters: GNASB (Ghana), FASB (US), ASB (UK) and the IASB (international).
  • Fundamental qualities: relevance and faithful representation. Enhancing qualities: comparability, understandability, timeliness (and verifiability).
  • Users: management, investors, lenders, employees, suppliers, customers, tax authorities and auditors.
  • Branches: financial, management and tax accounting. Financial accounting serves outsiders and looks at the past; management accounting serves insiders and looks to the future.

Week 2 — Business Documents

Every transaction begins with a source document that proves it happened.

Key points

  • Source documents are used to record transactions in the books.
  • Why needed: they give details of transactions, evidence for the audit, and proof for tax authorities.
  • Purchase order: asks a supplier for goods. No entry is made unless a deposit is paid.
  • Invoice: details of goods sold on credit; a sales invoice to the seller, a purchase invoice to the buyer.
  • Payment voucher: authorised document supporting payments; source for the credit side of the cash book.
  • Journal voucher: does the work of the journal proper and needs authorisation.
  • Cheque: an order to a bank to pay a stated sum on demand.
  • Bank statement: details the customer's account activity. Bank advice slips tell the customer of standing orders, interest and charges.

Week 3 — Accounting Concepts and Conventions

Accounting concepts and conventions are the ground rules for preparing accounts.

Key points

  • Going concern: the business will continue for the foreseeable future, so assets are not valued at break-up value.
  • Consistency: treat similar items the same way from period to period.
  • Accruals (matching): record revenue when earned and expenses when incurred; profit is revenue less the expenses used to earn it.
  • Prudence: be on the side of safety, but do not deliberately understate profit.
  • Substance over form: account for economic reality, not legal form.
  • Business entity: the business is separate from the owner.
  • Money measurement: only items that can be measured in money are recorded.
  • Realisation: recognise profit when goods are delivered. Disclosure: include all information users need.
  • IAS 1: assets and liabilities cannot be offset unless an IFRS permits it.

Week 4 — Systems of Recording and the Accounting Equation

The accounting equation is the foundation of double entry.

Key points

  • Single entry records one aspect of a transaction; double entry records two.
  • Assets = Capital + Liabilities. Every transaction keeps it in balance.
  • Borrowing from the bank: cash and loan both rise. Buying inventory on credit: inventory and trade payables both rise.
  • Paying a creditor: bank and trade payables both fall. Paying expenses: cash and capital both fall.
  • Day books: the sales day book records credit sales (from sales invoices); the purchases day book records credit purchases.
  • Return inwards are goods returned by customers.

Week 4-5 — The Journal, Capital and Revenue Expenditure

The journal is where each transaction is first recorded in double-entry form.

Key points

  • Two stages: journal first, then ledger.
  • A journal entry shows the date, the accounts debited and credited, the amounts and a narration starting with 'Being'.
  • Capital introduced in cash: debit Cash, credit Capital. Furniture bought on credit: debit Furniture, credit the supplier.
  • Capital expenditure benefits the business for more than one year and goes on the balance sheet.
  • Revenue expenditure benefits one accounting year and goes to the profit and loss account.
  • Examples: repairs are revenue; buying a motor van is capital.
  • Capital receipts include owner's contributions and loans.

Week 5 — The Ledger and the Cash Book

The ledger groups all transactions by account, and the cash book records cash and bank.

Key points

  • A ledger contains accounts, and entries are posted from the books of original entry.
  • Debit assets and expenses when they increase; credit liabilities, capital and income when they increase. Drawings are debited.
  • Balancing an account: find the difference, carry it down (c/d) and bring it down (b/d) on the opposite side.
  • The cash book is both a book of original entry and a ledger account.
  • Types: single column, two-column (cash and discount) and three-column (discount, cash and bank).
  • Discount allowed is on the debit side; discount received is on the credit side.
  • A contra entry transfers money between cash and bank.

Week 6 — The Trial Balance and Errors

The trial balance checks that debits equal credits.

Key points

  • A trial balance lists ledger balances at a point in time.
  • Purposes: check equality of debits and credits, summarise the ledger, and provide the basis for final accounts.
  • Errors that do not affect it: omission, compensating errors, reversal of entries, errors of principle, errors of commission, errors of original entry.
  • Errors that affect it, such as one-sided posting or wrong addition, are corrected through a suspense account.
  • Suspense account: temporary account used when the trial balance does not agree.
  • A trial balance that agrees does not prove the accounts are free of error.

Weeks 7-8 — Petty Cash and Bank Reconciliation

Petty cash deals with small payments, and a bank reconciliation explains differences between the cash book and the bank statement.

Key points

  • Petty cash book records numerous small payments, handled by a petty cashier.
  • Imprest system: the cashier gets a fixed float and is reimbursed for exactly the amount spent.
  • Bank reconciliation explains differences between the cash book and bank statement, and reduces the chance of fraud.
  • Reasons for differences: timing, items one side does not know about, errors.
  • Unpresented cheques: issued but not yet paid by the bank. Uncleared lodgements: paid in but not yet credited.
  • Items missing from the cash book: bank charges, standing orders, direct debits, direct receipts.
  • First adjust the cash book, then reconcile: bank balance + uncleared lodgements - unpresented cheques = adjusted cash book balance.

Weeks 9-10 — Depreciation and Disposal of Fixed Assets

Depreciation spreads the cost of a fixed asset over its useful life.

Key points

  • Fixed assets are acquired for use, not resale.
  • Causes: wear and tear, passage of time, obsolescence.
  • Reasons for charging: allocate cost, find true profit, show true position, provide for replacement.
  • Straight line: (cost - scrap value) / estimated useful life.
  • Reducing balance: a fixed percentage of the net book value each year, so the charge falls.
  • Net book value = cost less accumulated depreciation.
  • Disposal: transfer cost and accumulated depreciation to a disposal account; compare proceeds with NBV to find profit or loss.

Weeks 11-12 — Final Accounts

Final accounts show the result of trading and the financial position of the business.

Key points

  • Five components: statement of profit or loss, statement of financial position, cash flows, changes in equity, notes.
  • Trading account: gross profit = sales - cost of goods sold.
  • Cost of goods sold = opening stock + purchases - purchases returns + direct expenses - closing stock.
  • Profit and loss account: net profit = gross profit + other income - expenses.
  • Adjust for prepayments and accruals so expenses match the period.
  • Closing stock appears in the trading account and as a current asset.
  • Drawings are not an expense; they reduce capital.
