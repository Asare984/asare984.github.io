Week 1 — Overview of Business Finance

The big ideaBusiness finance is about how a firm raises money, how it uses it, and how it makes sure the owners end up richer.

The four decisions

Every finance manager makes four kinds of decision. Investment: what to buy (assets) to create value. Financing (capital structure): where the money comes from, such as owners' money or loans. Dividend policy: how much profit to pay out and how much to keep. Working capital: managing day-to-day money, such as cash, stock and what customers owe.

Example: a bakery decides to buy an oven (investment), borrows from the bank to pay for it (financing), pays the owner GH¢5,000 from profit and keeps the rest (dividend), and keeps enough cash for flour (working capital).

The goal of the firm

The accepted goal is wealth maximisation: increase the value of the owners' investment over the long term. Profit maximisation is not the best goal because it ignores timing (the time value of money), ignores risk, and focuses on the short term.

Example: Project A gives GH¢100,000 profit next year with low risk. Project B gives GH¢110,000 in ten years with high risk. Profit alone says B is better, but wealth maximisation says A is better.

Forms of business

A sole proprietorship has one owner who has unlimited liability (the owner's personal property can be taken to pay debts). A partnership has two or more owners. A corporation (company) is a separate legal person: owners have limited liability, the company has an unlimited life, and shares are easy to transfer.

Example: if a sole trader's shop owes GH¢50,000 and its assets are worth GH¢20,000, the trader may have to use personal savings for the rest. A shareholder in a company only risks the money invested.

The agency problem

In a company, shareholders (the principals) own it but managers (the agents) run it. Their goals can differ: managers might want big perks while shareholders want value. This conflict is the agency problem.

Solutions: give managers stock options or bonuses linked to share value, and have boards and auditors monitor them.

Quick recap

  • Four decisions: investment, financing, dividend, working capital
  • Goal: maximise wealth, not just profit
  • Sole trader = unlimited liability; company = limited liability
  • Agency problem = owners vs managers

Week 2 — Introduction to Financial Markets

The big ideaFinancial markets are places where people with spare money (savers) meet people who need money (borrowers).

Financial intermediaries

An intermediary links lenders and borrowers, usually by taking deposits and lending them out. Banks, pension funds and insurance companies are examples.

Example: you deposit GH¢1,000 in a bank; the bank lends it to a trader. You earn interest, the trader gets funds, and the bank earns the difference.

Primary and secondary markets

The primary market is where new securities are sold for the first time, so the money goes to the company. The secondary market is where existing securities are traded between investors, so the company does not receive the money.

Example: a company issuing new shares to the public (primary). You selling your shares to another investor on the stock exchange (secondary).

Money market and capital market

The money market trades short-term instruments (under a year), such as treasury bills and commercial paper. The capital market trades long-term instruments such as shares and bonds.

Example: a 91-day treasury bill is money market. A 10-year bond is capital market.

Commercial paper

Commercial paper is an unsecured promissory note with a fixed maturity, issued by large, reliable companies to raise short-term money.

Example: a big company borrows GH¢10 million for 90 days by issuing commercial paper.

Arbitrage

Arbitrage is buying something cheaply in one market and selling it at a higher price in another at the same time, for a risk-free profit.

Example: a share costs GH¢10 on one exchange and GH¢10.50 on another. Buy at 10, sell at 10.50, and keep GH¢0.50 per share. Such gaps close quickly.

Efficient markets

An efficient market is one where prices quickly reflect information. Weak form: prices reflect all past prices, so studying price charts cannot beat the market. Semi-strong form: prices reflect all public information. Strong form: prices reflect all information, even private.

Example: when a company announces higher profits, in a semi-strong efficient market its price rises immediately, so you cannot profit from the news after it is published.

Quick recap

  • Intermediaries link savers and borrowers
  • Primary = new issues; secondary = trading existing securities
  • Money market short-term; capital market long-term
  • Arbitrage = risk-free profit from price gaps
  • Weak, semi-strong, strong efficiency

Week 3 — Sources of Finance

The big ideaA firm can raise money in many ways. Choosing the right one depends on how long the money is needed, the cost, and the risk.

Overdraft

An overdraft lets you spend more than you have in your account up to a limit. Interest is charged only on the amount overdrawn and only for the days it is overdrawn. It is flexible but can be recalled by the bank at any time (repayable on demand).

Example: a shop with GH¢2,000 overdraft limit uses GH¢800 for a week and pays interest only on that GH¢800.

Trade credit

A supplier lets you take goods now and pay later, typically after 30 to 90 days. It is effectively an interest-free short-term loan, but you may lose early-payment discounts.

Example: a retailer buys GH¢10,000 of goods on 60 days' credit and sells them before paying.

Leasing and sale and leaseback

In a lease, the lessor (owner) keeps ownership while the lessee uses the asset and pays rentals. In a sale and leaseback, a firm sells a property it owns to get cash immediately and then rents it back.

Example: a company sells its office building to an insurance company for GH¢5 million and rents it back, so it gets cash but keeps using the building.

Debentures and bonds

These are long-term loans. The lender receives interest and the principal is repaid at the end.

Example: a company issues GH¢100 bonds paying 10% interest a year for 5 years.

Venture capital

Venture capital is risk money invested in a private business in exchange for part ownership (equity). It suits young firms with high growth potential that banks consider too risky.

Example: an investor puts GH¢500,000 into a new tech start-up for 30% of the company.

Equity (ordinary shares)

Ordinary shareholders are the owners. They carry the most risk: in a liquidation they are paid last, after all creditors. In return they may share in the profits and have voting rights.

Example: if a company is liquidated, bank loans are paid first and shareholders only get what remains.

Ways to raise equity

An IPO offers new shares to the general public for the first time. A placing sells shares to a few chosen institutions, so it is cheaper and quicker. A rights issue offers new shares to existing shareholders, usually at a discount. A Stock Exchange introduction gets an already widely-held company listed without selling new shares.

Example: a company gives each existing shareholder the right to buy one new share for every four they own, at a price below the market price.

Quick recap

  • Overdraft: flexible, interest on amount used
  • Trade credit: 30 to 90 days, interest-free
  • Lease keeps ownership with lessor
  • Equity holders bear most risk
  • IPO, placing, rights issue, introduction

Week 4 — Introduction to Capital Structure

The big ideaCapital structure is the mix of debt and equity a firm uses. Gearing measures how much debt the firm has compared with equity.

What gearing is

Gearing (leverage) compares debt with equity. If debt is greater than equity, the firm is highly geared. A highly geared firm has large fixed interest payments, so its profits to shareholders swing more.

Example: debt GH¢9m, equity GH¢6m. Gearing = 9 / 6 = 150%, so highly geared.

Three firms compared

Firm A has equity GH¢10m and debt GH¢2m, so gearing is 2/10 = 20% (low). Firm B has equity GH¢6m and debt GH¢9m, so gearing is 150% (high). Firm C has equity GH¢5m and debt GH¢5m, so gearing is 100% (neutral).

Remember: the higher the gearing, the higher the financial risk.

Modigliani and Miller (no tax)

In a perfect world with no taxes and no transaction costs, M&M say the value of the firm and its WACC do not depend on its mix of debt and equity. What matters is the quality of its investments.

Example: whether a company is financed 20% debt or 80% debt, its total value stays the same in this theory.

Modigliani and Miller (with tax)

When companies get tax relief on interest, debt creates a tax shield: interest reduces taxable profit. So debt lowers WACC and raises firm value, and the theory suggests more debt is always better.

Example: a firm pays GH¢100,000 interest at 25% tax. It saves GH¢25,000 in tax.

Traditional view

The traditional view says WACC first falls as cheap debt is added, then rises as higher gearing makes both debt and equity more expensive. So there is an optimal capital structure where WACC is lowest.

Remember: some debt is good; too much is dangerous.

Trade-off theory

Firms balance the tax benefit of debt against the costs of financial distress and bankruptcy. The best level of debt is where the two balance.

Example: a firm borrows more until the extra risk of failing outweighs the extra tax saving.

Pecking order theory

Firms prefer to finance in a set order: first retained earnings (cheapest and easiest), then debt, and only last new equity.

Example: a firm with spare profit uses it first, borrows if that is not enough, and issues shares only as a last resort.

Quick recap

  • Gearing = debt / equity
  • Over 100% highly geared
  • M&M no tax: structure irrelevant
  • M&M with tax: tax shield lowers WACC
  • Trade-off: tax benefit vs distress costs
  • Pecking order: retained earnings, debt, equity

Week 5 — Introduction to Financial Statement Analysis

The big ideaFinancial ratios turn the numbers in financial statements into simple measures that tell you whether a business is healthy.

Why ratios

Raw figures are hard to compare. Ratios let you compare a firm over time or against others. They fall into five groups: liquidity, leverage, asset management, profitability and market value.

Example: GH¢50,000 profit means little until you know whether it comes from GH¢500,000 or GH¢5,000,000 of sales.

Common-size statements

These express every balance sheet item as a percentage of total assets and every income statement item as a percentage of sales, so you can compare firms of different sizes.

Example: if inventory is GH¢300,000 and total assets are GH¢1,500,000, inventory is 20% of assets.

Liquidity ratios

Liquidity means the ability to pay short-term bills. Current ratio = current assets / current liabilities. Quick ratio = (current assets - inventory) / current liabilities, because inventory is the hardest current asset to turn into cash.

See the worked example below.

Leverage ratios

These show how much the firm uses debt. Total debt ratio = total debt / total assets. Times interest earned = EBIT / interest, showing how many times profit covers interest.

Example: a debt ratio of 0.36 means 36% of the assets are financed by debt.

Asset management ratios

Inventory turnover shows how many times stock is sold per year. Days' sales in inventory = 365 / turnover.

Example: turnover of 4 means 365 / 4 = 91.25 days to sell the average stock.

Profitability and market ratios

Return on equity (ROE) = net income / equity. Return on assets (ROA) = net income / total assets. If ROE is higher than ROA, the firm is using debt. P/E = share price / earnings per share.

Example: EPS GH¢0.60 and price GH¢5.25 gives P/E = 8.75, meaning investors pay GH¢8.75 for each GH¢1 of annual earnings.

Worked example: Madina Corporation

  1. Current assets = GH¢700,000; current liabilities = GH¢300,000; inventory = GH¢300,000
  2. Current ratio = 700 / 300 = 2.33 times
  3. Quick ratio = (700 - 300) / 300 = 1.33 times
  4. EBIT = 270; interest = 70, so times interest earned = 270 / 70 = 3.86 times
  5. Net income = 120; equity = 1,076, so ROE = 120 / 1,076 = 11.15%
  6. Net income 120,000 / 200,000 shares = EPS GH¢0.60; price 5.25 / 0.60 = P/E 8.75

Quick recap

  • Five groups of ratios
  • Current = CA / CL; Quick removes inventory
  • Times interest earned = EBIT / interest
  • ROE > ROA means debt is used
  • P/E = price / EPS

Week 6 — Time Value of Money

The big ideaA cedi today is worth more than a cedi next year, because today's cedi can be invested and earn interest. This is the time value of money.

Simple and compound interest

Simple interest is earned only on the original amount. Compound interest is earned on the original amount plus the interest already earned, so it grows faster.

Example: GH¢1,000 at 10% for 4 years. Simple: 1,000 + (1,000 x 10% x 4) = 1,400. Compound: 1,000 x 1.1^4 = 1,464.10.

Future value

Future value is what money today will be worth later. FV = PV x (1 + r)^n, where r is the interest rate and n the number of periods.

Example: GH¢10,000 at 12% for 3 years = 10,000 x 1.12^3 = 14,049.28.

Present value

Present value is what a future amount is worth today. PV = FV / (1 + r)^n. This is called discounting.

Example: GH¢5,000 due in 2 years at 8% is worth 5,000 / 1.08^2 = 4,286.69 today.

Nominal and real rates

The Fisher effect says (1 + nominal) = (1 + real) x (1 + inflation). So real rate = (1 + nominal) / (1 + inflation) - 1.

Example: nominal 6%, inflation 3%. Real = 1.06 / 1.03 - 1 = 2.91%.

Compounding more often

The more often interest is added, the higher the effective annual rate (EAR). EAR = (1 + nominal/m)^m - 1, where m is the number of times per year.

Example: 12% compounded monthly gives (1.01)^12 - 1 = 12.68%.

Annuities and perpetuities

An annuity is equal payments each period for a fixed time. A perpetuity pays forever; its present value is payment / rate.

Example: GH¢1,000 at the end of each of 3 years at 10% has PV = 2,486.85. A perpetuity of GH¢500 a year at 10% is worth 500 / 0.10 = 5,000.

Worked example: GH¢26,000 at 5% for 10 years

  1. Quarterly: 26,000 x (1 + 0.05/4)^40 = GH¢42,734.11
  2. Monthly: 26,000 x (1 + 0.05/12)^120 = GH¢42,822.25
  3. Daily: 26,000 x (1 + 0.05/365)^3650 = GH¢42,865.29
  4. Result: the more frequent the compounding, the more you earn

Quick recap

  • FV = PV(1 + r)^n
  • PV = FV / (1 + r)^n
  • EAR = (1 + nominal/m)^m - 1
  • Perpetuity PV = payment / rate

Week 7 — Introduction to Investment Decisions

The big ideaCapital budgeting is how a firm decides whether a big, long-term investment is worth doing.

Capital expenditure

Money spent on assets that will benefit the firm for more than one year, such as machinery or a new shop. It is shown on the balance sheet, not treated as an ordinary expense.

Example: buying a delivery van.

Payback period

Payback is how long it takes for the cash coming in to repay the cost. It is simple but ignores the time value of money and ignores cash flows after payback.

Example: cost GH¢10,000, inflows GH¢4,000 a year. After 2 years you have 8,000. The remaining 2,000 needs half of year 3, so payback = 2.5 years.

Net present value (NPV)

NPV discounts all future cash flows to today's money and subtracts the cost. If NPV is positive, the project adds value, so accept it.

Example: cost GH¢1,000, inflows GH¢600 for 3 years, discount rate 10%. PV of inflows = 600 x 2.4869 = 1,492.11. NPV = 1,492.11 - 1,000 = +492.11, so accept.

Internal rate of return (IRR)

IRR is the discount rate that makes the NPV zero. If IRR is higher than the required return (cost of capital), accept the project.

Example: the same project has NPV +171.20 at 25% and -46.65 at 40%, so IRR is about 36.31%. If the required return is 10%, accept.

Profitability index (PI)

PI = present value of inflows / initial investment. Accept if PI is greater than 1.

Example: 1,492.11 / 1,000 = 1.49, so accept.

MIRR

The modified IRR assumes cash inflows are reinvested at the firm's required rate of return, which is more realistic than IRR's assumption that they are reinvested at the IRR itself.

Remember: MIRR avoids overstating projects with very high IRRs.

Quick recap

  • Payback ignores time value
  • NPV > 0 accept
  • IRR > required return accept
  • PI > 1 accept
  • MIRR reinvests at the required return

Weeks 8-9 — Introduction to Risk and Return

The big ideaReturn is what you earn on an investment. Risk is the chance that you will earn something different from what you expected.

Holding period return

HPR = ending value / beginning value (including any income). The yield is HPR - 1.

Example: invest GH¢200 and get GH¢220 back. HPR = 220 / 200 = 1.10, so yield = 10%.

Total return

The return on a share comes from dividends plus capital gain (the increase in price).

Example: you buy at GH¢10, receive GH¢0.50 dividend and sell at GH¢11. Total return = (0.50 + 1) / 10 = 15%.

Expected return

Expected return is the average of possible returns, weighted by their probabilities.

Example: 20% with probability 0.3, 10% with 0.5, -5% with 0.2: 0.3x20 + 0.5x10 + 0.2x(-5) = 6 + 5 - 1 = 10%.

Measuring risk

Risk is measured by variance and standard deviation (SD), which show how far returns are likely to be from the expected return. The coefficient of variation (CV) = SD / expected return, which shows risk per unit of return.

Example: variance = 0.3(20-10)^2 + 0.5(10-10)^2 + 0.2(-5-10)^2 = 30 + 0 + 45 = 75, so SD = 8.66%. CV = 8.66 / 10 = 0.87.

Portfolio return

A portfolio's expected return is the weighted average of its assets' expected returns.

Example: 60% in an asset returning 10% and 40% in one returning 15% gives 0.6x10 + 0.4x15 = 12%.

Diversification

Markowitz showed that combining assets whose returns do not move perfectly together reduces risk. If the correlation is 1, SD is just the weighted average. If it is below 1, the portfolio's SD is lower.

Example: with SDs of 18.2% and 27.3% at 60%/40% and correlation 1, SD = 0.6x18.2 + 0.4x27.3 = 21.84%. With lower correlation it would be less.

Quick recap

  • HPR = end / start
  • Expected return = sum of probability x return
  • Risk: SD and variance; CV = SD / return
  • Diversification works when correlation < 1

Week 10 — Cost of Sources of Capital and WACC

The big ideaThe cost of capital is the return investors demand for providing money to the firm. It is the minimum return a project must earn.

Why cost of capital matters

A firm funded by shareholders, preference shareholders and lenders must earn at least what they expect. If a project earns less, the firm destroys value.

Example: if investors demand 16% overall, a project earning 12% should be rejected.

Cost of equity: constant dividend

If dividends stay the same forever, Ke = D / P0.

Example: dividend GH¢120, price GH¢800. Ke = 120 / 800 = 15%.

Cost of equity: growing dividend

If dividends grow at g each year, Ke = D1 / P0 + g, where D1 = D0 x (1 + g).

Example: D0 = 200, g = 10%, P0 = 1,200. D1 = 220. Ke = 220 / 1,200 + 0.10 = 28.33%.

Cum dividend and ex dividend

A share bought cum dividend entitles the buyer to the next dividend. A share bought ex dividend does not.

Example: buy cum div to receive tomorrow's dividend.

Cost of debt

Irredeemable debt: kd = interest x (1 - tax) / market price. Redeemable debt: the cost is the IRR of its cash flows.

Example: GH¢100 bond, 10% interest, price GH¢95, tax 25%. kd = 10 x 0.75 / 95 = 7.89%.

WACC

The weighted average cost of capital combines each source's cost, weighted by its share of total capital (preferably using market values).

See the worked example below.

Worked example: WACC

  1. Equity = GH¢600 at 20%; debt = GH¢400 at 10% after tax
  2. Total capital = 1,000
  3. WACC = (600 x 20% + 400 x 10%) / 1,000 = (120 + 40) / 1,000 = 16%
  4. Use 16% as the discount rate for projects with similar risk

Quick recap

  • Ke = D/P (constant) or D1/P0 + g (growing)
  • kd = i(1 - t)/price
  • WACC = weighted average of costs
  • Use WACC to discount projects

Week 11 — Introduction to Dividend Policy

The big ideaDividend policy answers a simple question: should the company pay profit to shareholders now, or keep it for the business?

Retained earnings

Retained earnings are profit the company keeps. They are not free: shareholders could have used the money elsewhere, so there is an opportunity cost. They avoid issue costs and do not dilute control.

Example: a company with GH¢1 million profit keeps GH¢600,000 for a new factory and pays GH¢400,000 in dividends.

Residual theory

First invest in all positive-NPV projects, then pay dividends from whatever is left, because external finance is more expensive.

Example: if profit is GH¢1 million and good projects need GH¢800,000, the dividend is GH¢200,000.

M&M irrelevance

In a perfect market with no taxes and no transaction costs, dividend policy does not affect firm value. Shareholders can create their own 'dividend' by selling a few shares.

Remember: in theory, investors are indifferent between dividends and capital gains.

Signalling and clientele

In the real world dividends can send messages. Cutting a dividend may signal cash flow problems. The clientele effect says some shareholders prefer dividends and others prefer growth, so they choose companies that match.

Example: retirees may prefer shares paying regular dividends.

Dividend policies

Constant payout ratio: pay a fixed percentage of net income. Stable dividend: pay a steady cedi amount. Zero dividend: keep everything for growth.

Example: 40% of GH¢120,000 net income = GH¢48,000 dividend.

Scrip dividends and buy-backs

A scrip dividend pays shareholders with extra shares instead of cash. A share repurchase is when a company buys back its own shares.

Example: a shareholder with 100 shares receives 5 more shares instead of cash.

Quick recap

  • Retained earnings have an opportunity cost
  • Residual theory: invest first, then pay
  • M&M: dividend policy irrelevant in perfect markets
  • Signalling and clientele effects
  • Constant payout, stable, zero dividend
