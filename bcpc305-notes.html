Week 1

Foundations of Leadership

In short: Leadership is one person influencing a group of people so they reach a goal together. It happens at four levels, it has clear functions, and it is not the same thing as management.

What is leadership?

Scholars define it differently, but the ideas agree. Leadership is a process (it happens over time, between people), not just a personality or a job title. Some definitions in your slides:

  • Northouse: a process where one person influences a group to achieve a common goal.
  • Kouzes and Posner: a relationship between those who aspire to lead and those who choose to follow.
  • Rauch and Behling: influencing the activities of an organised group towards goal achievement.

Northouse says almost every definition has four parts: process, influence, group context and goal attainment. Lussier and Achua list six elements: organisational objectives, people, process, influence, change, and leaders and followers.

Example: A course rep who gets the class to agree on a date for a group assignment and makes sure everyone submits is leading. There is a process (discussion), influence (persuading), a group (the class) and a goal (submission).

Levels of leadership

LevelWhat it means
PersonalLeading yourself: discipline, integrity and growth. Others learn from how you behave.
Direct (group)Leading a team towards a shared goal. The leader coordinates, motivates and settles disputes.
Organisational / managerialInfluencing the performance of the whole organisation. Needs technical, social, conceptual and spiritual competence.
StrategicLooking far ahead, seeing big opportunities, gathering resources and creating value for society. Needs vision, courage, resilience and discipline.

Functions of a leader

  • Inspire a shared vision.
  • Create followers who become leaders themselves.
  • Bring constructive change that serves the long-term needs of everyone involved.
  • Solve problems and settle conflicts.
  • Gather the resources needed to achieve the vision.

Leadership vs management

ManagementLeadership
Doing things right (efficiency)Doing the right things (effectiveness)
Plans, organises, directs, controlsSets vision, inspires, coaches, empowers
Short-term view, asks "how and when"Long-term view, asks "what and why"
Accepts the status quoChallenges the status quo
Position comes from time and loyalty in the organisationPeople follow by their own choice

They overlap. Leadership is one part of the directing function of management, and a manager also needs formal authority to be effective.

Remember this
  • Leadership = influence + group + shared goal, as a process.
  • Four levels: personal, direct, organisational, strategic.
  • Management is doing things right. Leadership is doing the right things.

Check yourself

Define leadership and name the four components Northouse identified.
Leadership is a process in which an individual influences a group to achieve a common goal. The four components are process, influence, group context and goal attainment.
Give two differences between a leader and a manager.
A manager asks "how and when", keeps to the status quo and takes a short-term view. A leader asks "what and why", challenges the status quo and takes a long-term view.

Week 2

Theories of Leadership

In short: A theory is a framework that helps explain why some people become leaders and what makes them effective. This week covers five: Great Man, Trait, Managerial Grid, Pygmalion and Golem.

Why do we study theories?

A theory is a set of ideas that explains something. A leadership theory explains why and how certain people become leaders, and which traits, behaviours and situations make leaders effective.

1. Great Man Theory

The oldest idea: leaders are born, not made. It became popular in the 19th century. People believed great leaders such as Abraham Lincoln and Julius Caesar had natural, inborn abilities that ordinary people did not have.

Criticism: Herbert Spencer argued that leaders are shaped by the society and times they live in. It also ignores the fact that leadership skills can be learnt.

2. Trait Theory

The next step: instead of "great men", look at the traits (steady patterns of behaviour, like confidence or persistence) that leaders share. Researchers such as Stogdill, Kirkpatrick and Locke, and Zaccaro compiled lists of traits.

The core traits that keep appearing:

  • Intelligence: leaders tend to be higher in reasoning and problem solving.
  • Integrity: honesty and doing what you say you will do.
  • Sociability: seeking good relationships with people.
  • Self-confidence, drive and persistence: believing in yourself and staying with the goal.

Criticisms (a favourite exam topic):

  • No single list of traits works for every leader, and the lists keep growing.
  • The choice of "important" traits is subjective, based on each author's views.
  • It ignores the situation. Height and weight may matter in the army but not in a business.
  • Many people have the traits yet never lead.

The Leadership Trait Questionnaire (LTQ) measures traits such as being articulate, perceptive, confident, persistent and empathic. You and five people who know you rate yourself from 1 to 5.

3. Managerial (Leadership) Grid

Developed by Blake and Mouton (1964). It rates a leader on two things, each from 1 (low) to 9 (high): concern for production (tasks, results) and concern for people (needs, welfare). Together they give five styles.

Style (production, people)What it looks like
Impoverished (1,1)Cares about neither. Avoids responsibility. Low morale, poor results.
Country Club (1,9: low production, high people)Friendly and comfortable, but little gets done.
Task / Authority-Compliance (9,1: high production, low people)Strict and result-driven. Workers feel like tools, leading to burnout and turnover.
Middle-of-the-Road (5,5)Tries to balance both. Peace is kept but results are only average.
Team / Sound (9,9)High concern for both. Best style: teamwork, high morale and high performance.

Example: A school principal who never supervises because she wants teachers comfortable is Country Club. A factory supervisor who pushes quotas without thinking about tired workers is Task management.

4. Pygmalion effect

High expectations lead to high performance. If a leader truly believes a person can do well, the leader gives more encouragement, bigger tasks and better feedback. The person gains confidence and performs better, which confirms the belief. This is a self-fulfilling prophecy.

Example: A lecturer who believes a student is bright gives him challenging work and attention, and his results improve.

To use it: set high but realistic expectations, show belief in people, give constructive feedback and avoid labelling anyone as "weak" or "slow".

5. Golem effect

The opposite. Low expectations lead to poor performance. A leader who doubts someone gives them only routine tasks, watches them closely and ignores their ideas. The person loses confidence, performs poorly, and may even leave.

The five at a glance

TheoryCore idea
Great ManLeaders are born with natural abilities.
TraitCertain personality traits make effective leaders.
Managerial GridStyle depends on balance of concern for people vs production.
PygmalionHigh expectations produce high performance.
GolemLow expectations produce poor performance.
Remember this
  • Great Man = born. Trait = qualities. Grid = people vs production. Pygmalion = high expectation up. Golem = low expectation down.
  • Best grid style is Team Management (9,9).
  • Leadership is both an art and a science, and it can be developed.

Check yourself

State two criticisms of Trait Theory.
There is no universal list of traits that fits every leader, and the choice of traits is subjective. It also ignores the situation, and many people with the traits never become leaders.
What is the difference between the Pygmalion and Golem effects?
Under Pygmalion, a leader's high expectations raise a person's performance. Under Golem, a leader's low expectations lower it. Both are self-fulfilling prophecies.
Which grid style has high concern for production but low concern for people?
Task (Authority-Compliance) management. It gets short-term output but causes dissatisfaction, conflict and high staff turnover.

Week 3

Contingency Theories of Leadership

In short: "Contingency" means "it depends". No single leadership style works everywhere. The best style depends on the situation. Three theories: Fiedler, Hersey and Blanchard, and Path-Goal.

Why contingency theories came about

Trait theories said leaders are born. Behavioural theories said one style (autocratic, democratic or laissez-faire) is best. Experience showed both were incomplete. A democratic leader can fail in a crisis, and a strict leader can fail with skilled, motivated staff. So scholars in the 1960s added the situation.

1. Fiedler's Contingency Theory (1967)

Fiedler says leader effectiveness depends on matching the leader's style with how favourable the situation is. His big assumption: a leader's style is fixed. You cannot easily change it, so you should place the leader in the right situation.

Step 1: find the leader's style (LPC scale)

The leader thinks of the co-worker they found hardest to work with (the Least Preferred Co-worker) and rates them on pairs like friendly/unfriendly and cooperative/uncooperative.

  • High LPC (73 or above): still sees the person positively, so is relationship-oriented.
  • Low LPC (54 or below): rates the person harshly, so is task-oriented.
  • 55 to 72: can fit either style.

Example: Ama calls her difficult colleague "friendly but uncooperative". She focuses on the person, so she is high LPC. Kwesi calls him "lazy and annoying". He focuses on results, so he is low LPC.

Step 2: measure the situation with three factors

  • Leader-member relations: do followers trust and respect you? (good or poor)
  • Task structure: are the tasks clear and standardised? (structured or unstructured)
  • Position power: can you reward and punish? (strong or weak)

Good relations + structured task + strong power = very favourable. The opposite = very unfavourable.

Step 3: match

RelationsTaskPowerBest leader
GoodStructuredStrongTask (low LPC)
GoodStructuredWeakTask (low LPC)
GoodUnstructuredStrongTask (low LPC)
GoodUnstructuredWeakRelationship (high LPC)
PoorStructuredStrongRelationship (high LPC)
PoorStructuredWeakRelationship (high LPC)
PoorUnstructuredStrongRelationship (high LPC)
PoorUnstructuredWeakTask (low LPC)

Easy way to remember: task-oriented leaders do best when the situation is very good or very bad. Relationship-oriented leaders do best in the middle, where people skills make the difference.

Limitations: it assumes style cannot change (so you replace the leader instead), and the LPC measure can be confusing or unfair.

Fiedler's picture: leadership is like sailing. Success depends on the captain's skill and also on the wind and sea.

2. Hersey and Blanchard's Situational Leadership (1969)

Unlike Fiedler, they say a good leader can and should change style. The situation here means the followers' readiness: their competence (skill) and commitment (confidence and willingness).

Follower levelFollowers areLeader styleWhat the leader says
D1Unable and unwillingS1 Telling"Do this exactly as I say."
D2Unable but willingS2 Selling (coaching)"Let's do it this way, and here's why."
D3Able but unwilling or unsureS3 Participating"What do you think? Let's decide together."
D4Able and willingS4 Delegating"You've got this. I trust you."

Parent analogy: A very young child is told exactly what to do (telling). A learning, eager child gets instructions and encouragement (selling). A capable but unsure child is listened to (participating). A grown, capable child is trusted (delegating).

Strengths: simple, practical, focuses on developing followers. Limitations: "readiness" is vague and hard to measure, it ignores things like culture, and not every leader can switch styles easily.

3. Path-Goal Theory (Robert House, 1971)

Based on expectancy theory of motivation. The leader's job is to show followers the path to the goal, remove obstacles and make the journey rewarding. Think of the leader as a road guide.

StyleWhat the leader doesUse when
DirectiveGives clear instructions, sets standards, monitors progressTask is unclear or complex, followers are inexperienced
SupportiveShows care for followers' well-being, creates a friendly climateWork is stressful or tedious
ParticipativeAsks for suggestions before decidingFollowers are experienced and the task needs creativity
Achievement-orientedSets challenging goals and shows confidence in followersFollowers are competent and motivated

The style is chosen based on two sets of factors: follower characteristics (their perceived ability) and environment (task structure, work-group relationships).

The three compared

FiedlerHersey and BlanchardPath-Goal

StylesTask or relationshipTelling, selling, participating, delegatingDirective, supportive, participative, achievement
Remember this
  • Contingency means leadership effectiveness depends on the situation.
  • Fiedler: style is fixed, so match the leader to the situation. The other two: change your style to fit.
  • Fiedler's three factors: relations, task structure, position power.

Check yourself

What does a high LPC score tell us about a leader?
The leader is relationship-oriented. They still rate their least preferred co-worker favourably and put relationships before tasks.
Which Hersey and Blanchard style fits a new, willing intern with little skill?
S2 Selling (coaching). The intern is unable but willing (D2), so the leader gives direction and encouragement.
Name the four Path-Goal leadership styles.
Directive, supportive, participative and achievement-oriented.

Week 4

Leadership in Practice and Governance

In short: Leadership looks different in a classroom, a company, a community and a government, but the same core ideas apply: lead by example, keep learning, care about society, shape the culture, and be accountable.

Leadership in varied contexts

"Varied contexts" means different settings. Leadership is needed at work, at school, in the community and in a sports team. The approach must be adapted to each setting.

Example: In a school, the course rep is the leader who speaks for the class and organises it. The same person would lead differently on a football team.

Leadership challenges: the five practices of exemplary leaders

Kouzes and Posner collected stories of people at their leadership best. They found that great leadership is about behaviours you can learn, not personality. Five practices came up again and again:

  1. Model the way: set an example by living the values you expect from others.
  2. Inspire a shared vision: picture a better future and get people excited about it.
  3. Challenge the process: look for chances to improve things and accept that some risks are needed.
  4. Enable others to act: build teamwork and trust, and involve people.
  5. Encourage the heart: recognise people's contributions so they keep going. Make them feel like heroes.

Leadership opportunities

You do not need a big title to lead. Examples: leading a project at work, volunteering to lead a club or community group, starting your own business, or organising a family event.

Leadership development

Leaders keep growing. Key areas to develop: decision-making, communication, emotional intelligence (understanding your emotions and other people's), conflict resolution and adaptability (leading through change).

Social responsibility

A leader must care about the well-being of the wider community, not only the organisation. This shows up as:

  • Ethical decision-making that considers employees, customers and the community.
  • Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) programmes, for example in education or health.
  • Transparency and accountability for the results of your decisions.
  • Obeying laws and regulations, and caring about sustainability.

Leadership and organisational culture

Culture means the shared beliefs, values and norms that guide how an organisation behaves. Leaders shape the culture, and culture shapes how leaders can lead.

TypeWhat it stresses
HierarchicalStrict chain of command, centralised decisions
CollegialTeamwork, collaboration, open communication
ClanLoyalty, closeness, a family-like atmosphere
Results-orientedReaching targets, competition, measurable outcomes
Customer-centricPutting customers at the centre of everything

Leadership in governance

Governance is the way power is used to manage a country's economic and social resources. Leaders shape policy and decisions. Good leadership in governance:

  • Promotes stability: less conflict and a predictable climate for investment. Example: respecting the rule of law and peaceful transfers of power.
  • Ensures accountability: leaders take responsibility for results, which builds public trust.
  • Enhances responsiveness: leaders listen to citizens and change policies when needs change.
  • Encourages participation: inclusive decision-making that involves citizens and civil society.

Poor leadership leads to instability and unrest, corruption, public apathy and distrust, and policy failure.

Remember this
  • Five practices: model the way, inspire a shared vision, challenge the process, enable others to act, encourage the heart.
  • Four governance principles: stability, accountability, responsiveness, participation.
  • Five culture types: hierarchical, collegial, clan, results-oriented, customer-centric.

Check yourself

List the five practices of exemplary leadership.
Model the way, inspire a shared vision, challenge the process, enable others to act, and encourage the heart.
Give two consequences of poor leadership in governance.
Any two of: instability and social unrest, corruption, public apathy and distrust, and policy failure.

Week 5

Trust and Power in Leadership

In short: Trust and power are the two things that let a leader influence people. Trust must be earned. Power can come from your position or from who you are, and good leaders rely more on the second kind.

What is trust?

Trust is a confident expectation that another person is honest, dependable and competent, and a willingness to be vulnerable to them because of that belief. A title does not give you trust automatically. It is not "pre-packaged with the nameplate". It is earned through your ability, consistency, integrity and delivery.

Where there is trust, teams do better: more satisfaction, loyalty, innovation and productivity. Where there is mistrust, there is doubt, frustration and lower output.

Dimensions of trust

Integrity, competence, consistency, loyalty and openness.

Types of trust

TypeMeaning
Competence trustBased on skill and ability. You may not like the person, but you trust them to get the job done.
Benevolence trustBased on good intentions. You believe the person looks out for you and will not undermine you.
Integrity trustBased on moral character. The person acts on principle regardless of who is involved.

How to build and keep trust

  • Clarity: people trust clarity and distrust confusion. Be clear about expectations.
  • Compassion: show that you really care about people's interests.
  • Character: do what is right ahead of what is easy.
  • Contribution: results build trust quickly.
  • Competency: stay current and keep learning. Arrogance kills confidence.
  • Connection: ask questions, listen and show gratitude.
  • Commitment: stand with people through hard times.
  • Consistency: small things done regularly make the big difference.

What destroys trust: self-orientation (putting your own interest above the team's) and lack of transparency (hiding information or motives).

What is power?

Power is the ability to influence others. It is the capacity of one party to influence another.

Types of power (French and Raven, extended by Hersey and Blanchard)

There are seven, grouped into two families.

FamilyTypeWhere it comes from
PositionalLegitimateYour position or title (formal authority)
RewardAbility to give pay rises, promotions, recognition
CoerciveAbility to punish or withhold rewards
PersonalExpertKnowledge and skill that others value
ReferentPeople admire you and want to be like you (charisma)
ConnectionWho you know, inside and outside the organisation
InformationHaving access to information others need (short-term power)

Leaders are generally better served using reward power than coercive power. Rewards and punishment both motivate, but fear damages trust.

Example: A lecturer has legitimate power (the position), reward power (marks) and coercive power (penalties). Students who follow him because they admire him are responding to referent power.

Remember this
  • Trust is earned, not given with a title.
  • Seven types of power: legitimate, reward, coercive (positional) and expert, referent, connection, information (personal).
  • Trust and power work together: power gets compliance, trust gets commitment.

Check yourself

Distinguish between positional and personal power.
Positional power comes from the office you hold (legitimate, reward, coercive). Personal power comes from you as a person (expert, referent, connection, information).
Why does holding a high position not guarantee trust?
Trust is earned through competence, integrity, consistency and delivery. A title only gives authority.

Week 6

Traditional Leadership

In short: Traditional leadership in Ghana is the chieftaincy system, based on custom and lineage. The 1992 Constitution recognises it, and Parliament cannot take that recognition away.

What is traditional leadership?

It is leadership where authority is tied to tradition and custom. A traditional leader comes from the appropriate family and lineage and is validly chosen and installed (enstooled or enskinned) according to custom. Chiefs and queen mothers are the main holders. Divisional and sub-divisional chiefs, headmen and elders also belong. They exist in all 16 regions. The institution in charge is the National House of Chiefs (NHC), under the Chieftaincy Act 1971 (Act 370).

A short history

  • Before colonialism there were two systems: a centralised one with a king or paramount chief at the top, and a decentralised one without a single ruler.
  • The colonial rulers recognised both and used chiefs for indirect rule, and also added a judicial role to the chiefs' roles.
  • The colonial arrangement is blamed for some modern chieftaincy conflicts, and some chiefs dislike the word "chief" because it was introduced in colonial times.

How it is organised (1992 Constitution)

ArticleWhat it says
270Recognises the institution of chieftaincy and traditional councils. Parliament cannot make a law that gives or takes away a chief's recognition.
271Sets up the National House of Chiefs, with paramount chiefs from each region.
273The NHC has appellate jurisdiction. It handles matters on chieftaincy appealed from Regional Houses through a Judicial Committee.
274Each region has a Regional House of Chiefs.
275Disqualifies a person from being a chief if convicted of high treason, a high crime, or offences like fraud, dishonesty or immorality. Chiefs must stay out of active party politics.

Other bodies: the Council of State (advises the President) and the Ministry for Chieftaincy and Traditional Affairs, which works with the NHC on studying and recording customary law.

Levels (top to bottom): Ministry, National House of Chiefs, Council of State, Regional Houses of Chiefs, then districts, towns, clans and villages.

Roles of chiefs

Chiefs have always had political, social and military roles. The British found them in firm control of their people and so added a judicial role. Today chiefs are mostly development partners: they mobilise communities and land and push local development.

Chieftaincy today: the debate

  • Democracy stresses equality, free speech and the rule of law, so some ask whether chiefs still have a place.
  • Some want the institution abolished, some say it is too costly, and some want it stripped of political power and left to custom only.
  • Others argue that chiefs have been the weaker partner in governance, and that they should have a clearer role, such as a place in a second chamber or restored judicial duties.
  • Newer forms exist: development chiefs, youth leaders, non-titled chiefs and migrant chiefs. Today people increasingly prefer chiefs who are educated and can bring development.
Remember this
  • Authority comes from custom and lineage. Chiefs are enstooled or enskinned.
  • Two pre-colonial systems: centralised and decentralised.
  • Article 270 protects chieftaincy. Article 275 sets disqualification rules and bars active party politics.

Check yourself

Explain the two traditional authority systems that existed before colonialism.
The centralised system had a clear hierarchy with a king or paramount chief at the top. The decentralised system had no single ruler at the apex.
State two grounds that disqualify someone from being a chief under Article 275.
Conviction for high treason or a high crime, or for offences involving state security, fraud, dishonesty or immorality.

Week 7

Leadership Styles and Skills

In short: A leadership style is the way a leader gives direction and motivates people. Skills are the abilities that make that style work. This week covers three classic styles, two modern styles, and four groups of skills.

The three classic styles (Kurt Lewin, 1939)

StyleHow it worksBest used when
Authoritarian (autocratic)The leader decides alone and gives clear orders. There is a clear gap between leader and followers.Time is short or the leader knows the most. Overused, it feels bossy and controlling.
Participative (democratic)The leader guides and takes part, and invites input. Lewin found it the most effective.You want ideas, motivation and good working relationships.
Laissez-faire (delegative)The leader gives little or no direction. Lewin found the group least productive.Only with highly skilled, self-driven people.

Transformational leadership

The leader inspires followers to go beyond their own interests, raises their motivation and paints a picture of the future. Bass (1985) gave four elements, often called the four I's:

  • Idealised influence: the leader is a charismatic role model.
  • Inspirational motivation: communicates a vision that makes people feel ownership.
  • Intellectual stimulation: encourages creativity and new ways of solving problems.
  • Individual consideration: pays attention to each follower's needs and ambitions.

Transactional leadership

An exchange: followers do the work, the leader gives something in return (quid pro quo). It is more about tasks and targets than people. Three parts:

  • Contingent reward: reward in exchange for achieving the result.
  • Management by exception (active): the leader watches closely and corrects mistakes, a kind of micromanagement.
  • Management by exception (passive): the leader gives space and only steps in when something goes wrong.

Skills for leadership

GroupExamples
AdministrativeProblem analysis (understanding how things work), judgment (making sound decisions from the information you have), organisational ability
InterpersonalCoaching (spotting and developing others' talents), sensitivity (understanding people's needs and handling conflicts discreetly)
CommunicationOral (speaking clearly), written (conveying ideas without confusion), non-verbal (body language)
Related skillsRange of interests, personal motivation, flexibility

Example: A group leader who explains the task clearly (oral communication), notices that one member is struggling (sensitivity) and helps him (coaching) is using skills from three groups at once.

Remember this
  • Lewin's three styles: authoritarian, democratic, laissez-faire.
  • Transformational inspires and develops people. Transactional exchanges rewards for results.
  • Four skill groups: administrative, interpersonal, communication, related skills.

Check yourself

Compare transformational and transactional leadership.
Transformational leaders inspire, develop followers and share a vision. Transactional leaders exchange rewards for performance and focus on tasks and rules.
When is the authoritarian style appropriate?
When there is little time for group decision-making, or when the leader is the most knowledgeable person in the group.

Week 9

Decision-Making Models and Styles

In short: A leader is judged by the decisions they make and how those decisions turn into results. This week covers how intelligence and experience affect decisions, how much to involve followers, and two ways of deciding: rational and incremental.

Cognitive Resource Model (Fiedler and Garcia, 1987)

It asks: when do a leader's intelligence and experience actually help? The answer depends on stress.

SituationWhat matters
Low stressIntelligence helps. Leaders can think things through and use their brains.
High stressIntelligence does little. Experience counts, because leaders fall back on what they have done before.

Intelligence also helps the team only when the leader is directive. For simple tasks, neither matters much.

Strength: it stresses intelligence and suggests checking that leaders can manage stress. Weakness: it does not define intelligence or the kinds of stress, and it ignores leaders with both high IQ and experience.

Vroom and Yetton's Normative Model (1973)

Leaders improve results by involving followers, but how much to involve them depends on the problem. A good decision needs two things: quality (a better choice) and acceptance (followers accept it as their own). Time also matters.

Five decision strategies

CodeTypeWhat the leader does
A1AutocraticDecides alone using the information at hand
A2AutocraticGets information from followers, then decides alone
C1ConsultativeShares the problem with followers one by one, then decides alone
C2ConsultativeShares the problem with followers as a group, then decides alone
G2GroupShares the problem with the group and decides together

Rules of thumb

  • If decision quality matters and followers have useful information, pure autocratic styles (A1, A2) are not the best choice.
  • If quality matters but followers do not share the organisation's goals, do not use G2.
  • If acceptance is critical and followers are likely to disagree with each other, avoid A1, A2 and C1. Let differences be heard.
  • If quality does not matter but acceptance does, G2 is best.

Rational vs incremental decisions

RationalIncremental (Lindblom)
ApproachIdentify the problem, list all alternatives, weigh costs and benefits, choose the bestMake small adjustments to what already exists
Used whenThere is time and informationTime, information or money is limited, or consequences cannot be predicted
AimHighest gain over costStability, agreement and less conflict

Incremental is common in public policy: "fine-tuning" existing programmes.

Factors that affect decision-making

Time or speed, structure of the task, number and calibre of people involved, subject matter, stakeholders, interests, experience and stress.

Remember this
  • Low stress: intelligence helps. High stress: experience helps.
  • A1, A2, C1, C2, G2: from deciding alone to deciding with the group.
  • Good decision = quality + acceptance.

Check yourself

Differentiate C1 from C2.
In both the leader consults and decides alone. In C1 the leader consults followers individually. In C2 the leader consults them as a group.
Why does the Cognitive Resource Model say experience matters more under high stress?
Under high stress, rational solutions are not always available, so intelligence does little. Experienced leaders can rely on past practice and get better results.

Week 10

Leading Teams and Groups, and Leading in Crisis

In short: A team is more than a group. Groups move through predictable stages, and a leader's job is to get them to performing quickly and keep them from the common traps. In a crisis, true leadership is tested.

Group vs team

GroupTeam
Two or more people who interact freely and share norms, goals and an identityA small group with complementary skills who hold themselves mutually accountable for a shared purpose, goals and approach

A group becomes a team when it has shared leadership, individual and collective accountability, a shared mission and results judged by the collective outcome.

Types of groups

  • Formal: created by the organisation. These are command groups (people reporting to one manager) and task groups (working on a specific job).
  • Informal: form naturally to meet social needs. These are interest groups and friendship groups.

Why people join groups: security, status, self-esteem, affiliation, power and goal achievement.

Group dynamics

  • Size: keep groups small (2 to 9). Small groups talk more, are more motivated and see their own contribution. Groups of 7 or 9 tend to do best, and odd numbers beat even numbers.
  • Large groups have communication problems, lower motivation, social loafing and groupthink, and they can suffocate innovation.
  • Social loafing: people put in less effort when working in a group than alone. Think of group assignments where one person does most of the work.
  • Groupthink: the wish for harmony pushes the group into poor decisions because no one wants to disagree.
  • Task interdependence: the more one person's work depends on another's, the closer they must work.

Five stages of group development (Tuckman)

StageWhat happens
FormingIce-breaking. Uncertainty and some anxiety. People learn the task and each other.
StormingConflict over roles and objectives. Dominant members emerge. Conflict must be handled to move on.
NormingCloseness and cohesion. Roles, rules and norms are set.
PerformingFully functional. Trust, focus and commitment. The real work happens.
AdjourningWrapping up. Giving recognition and closure, especially for temporary teams.

Groups can get stuck at a stage or fall back. The faster they reach performing, the better.

Types of work teams

Advice (broaden information for decisions), production (day-to-day operations), project (specialised knowledge for creative problem solving) and action (highly coordinated specialists, for example a surgical team).

Leading successful teams

Effective teams have: a clear purpose, participation, listening, civilised disagreement, consensus decisions, open communication, clear roles, clear values and shared leadership.

Why teams fail:

  • Management mistakes: weak strategy, a hostile environment, using teams as a quick fix, vague assignments, no training.
  • Team problems: unrealistic expectations, conflict over work styles, results-only focus, resistance to change, poor interpersonal skills, lack of trust.

To reduce social loafing: make each person's effort identifiable and accountable, stress each person's value, and keep the group an appropriate size. Make sure members benefit when the group performs well.

Leading a virtual team: start with a face-to-face meeting, set regular times to interact, set firm communication rules, use visual communication, give feedback regularly and be available.

Leading in crisis

A crisis is a specific, unexpected and non-routine event that creates high uncertainty and a real or perceived threat to an organisation's top goals (Seeger et al., 1998). A leader's true substance is tested in a crisis.

Remember this
  • Stages: forming, storming, norming, performing, adjourning.
  • Social loafing hurts large groups. Groupthink hurts decision quality.
  • A team is mutually accountable. A group is not necessarily.

Week 11

Ethical Leadership and Values

In short: Ethics is knowing right from wrong. Ethical leadership means making decisions ethically and also treating people ethically. Northouse gives five principles: respect, serve, be just, be honest and build community.

Ethics, morality and values

  • Ethics: principles for judging behaviour as right or wrong. The word comes from Greek ethos (character, custom).
  • Morality: the rules that guide conduct. It comes from Latin mores. The two words mean nearly the same.
  • Values: what a group or profession judges to be right. Ethics is behaving in line with those values.
  • Ethical dilemma: a situation where you must choose between equally unfavourable options.

The 1992 Constitution warns against unethical practices such as conflict of interest and illicit enrichment.

What is ethical leadership?

Leadership guided by respect for ethical beliefs and values and for the dignity and rights of others. It relates to trust, honesty, consideration and fairness. It needs two things:

  1. The leader acts and decides ethically.
  2. The leader leads ethically: how they treat people day to day.

Five principles of ethical leadership (Northouse)

PrincipleMeaning
Respect othersTreat people as having their own goals, never only as a means to your end.
Serve othersAct as a steward of the vision and put others before yourself.
Be justPut fairness at the centre of decisions. Nobody is treated differently unless their situation demands it.
Be honestMore than telling the truth: be open and represent reality fully.
Build communityPursue goals that leaders and followers share and that serve the wider good.

Ethical decision-making

It is about choice and balance, discarding bad choices in favour of good ones. A first question to ask: "What would a reasonable person do in this situation?" Shared values between leader and followers are the base of trust.

Who enforces ethics and accountability in Ghana?

CHRAJ, EOCO, the Economic Crime Court, the Public Accounts Committee, the Auditor-General, the Office of Accountability and the Special Prosecutor, among others.

To think about: What would you do if you were a judge in a robbery trial involving your own child? That is an ethical dilemma: loyalty versus justice.

Remember this
  • Five principles: respect, serve, just, honest, build community.
  • Ethical leaders both decide and lead ethically.
  • "The function of leadership is to produce more leaders, not more followers." (Ralph Nader)
