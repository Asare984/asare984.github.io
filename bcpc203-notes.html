Week 1 — Overview of Business Finance

Business finance is about the decisions that create value for owners.

Key points

  • Four decision areas: investment, financing, dividend policy and working capital management.
  • Investment decision: what assets to acquire. Financing decision (capital structure): how to raise the money.
  • Goal of the firm: wealth (value) maximisation, not profit maximisation.
  • Profit maximisation ignores the time value of money and risk and is short term.
  • Business forms: sole proprietorship (unlimited liability), partnership, corporation (limited liability, unlimited life, easy transfer of ownership).
  • Agency problem: conflict between shareholders (principals) and managers (agents). Solutions include stock options and bonuses.

Week 2 — Introduction to Financial Markets

Financial markets move money from savers to borrowers.

Key points

  • Intermediaries link lenders and borrowers.
  • Primary market: new issues. Secondary market: trading of existing securities.
  • Money market: short-term instruments (treasury bills, commercial paper). Capital market: long-term (shares, bonds).
  • Arbitrage: buying low in one market and selling high in another for a risk-free profit.
  • Efficient market forms: weak (past prices), semi-strong (all public information), strong (all information).

Week 3 — Sources of Finance

Firms raise finance from short-term, medium-term and long-term sources.

Key points

  • Short-term: overdraft (flexible, interest only when overdrawn) and trade credit (typically 30 to 90 days).
  • Leasing: the lessor keeps ownership and the lessee uses the asset. Sale and leaseback gives immediate cash.
  • Debentures and bonds: long-term debt.
  • Venture capital: risk capital for an equity stake in private firms.
  • Equity: ordinary shareholders bear the most risk. Methods: IPO, placing, rights issue, introduction.

Week 4 — Introduction to Capital Structure

Capital structure is the mix of debt and equity.

Key points

  • Gearing: debt relative to equity. Over 100% is highly geared; below 100% is low.
  • M&M without tax: capital structure does not affect firm value. With tax: debt lowers WACC through the tax shield.
  • Traditional view: WACC falls then rises, so there is an optimal gearing.
  • Trade-off theory: tax benefit of debt versus distress costs.
  • Pecking order: retained earnings first, then debt, then equity.

Week 5 — Introduction to Financial Statement Analysis

Ratios turn financial statements into useful comparisons.

Key points

  • Five groups: liquidity, leverage, asset management, profitability, market value.
  • Current ratio = current assets / current liabilities. Quick ratio = (current assets - inventory) / current liabilities.
  • Times interest earned = EBIT / interest.
  • Inventory days = 365 / inventory turnover.
  • ROE = net income / equity. If ROE exceeds ROA, debt is being used.
  • P/E = price / EPS. Common-size statements show items as a percentage of total assets or sales.

Week 6 — Time Value of Money

Money today is worth more than the same money in the future.

Key points

  • Simple interest: principal x rate x time. Compound interest earns interest on interest: FV = PV x (1 + r)^n.
  • PV = FV / (1 + r)^n.
  • Fisher effect: (1 + nominal) / (1 + inflation) - 1 = real rate.
  • Effective annual rate rises with more frequent compounding.
  • Annuity: equal payments each period. Perpetuity PV = payment / rate.

Week 7 — Introduction to Investment Decisions

Capital budgeting evaluates long-term investment projects.

Key points

  • Capital expenditure benefits the business for more than one year.
  • Payback: time to recover the outlay; ignores time value and later cash flows.
  • NPV: accept if positive. IRR: rate at which NPV is zero. MIRR assumes reinvestment at the required rate.
  • Profitability index = PV of inflows / initial investment; accept if above 1.

Weeks 8-9 — Introduction to Risk and Return

Higher expected returns usually come with higher risk.

Key points

  • Holding period return = ending value / beginning value; yield = HPR - 1.
  • Expected return = sum of probability x return.
  • Risk measured by variance and standard deviation. Coefficient of variation = SD / expected return.
  • Portfolio return is the weighted average of asset returns.
  • Diversification reduces risk when correlation is below 1.

Week 10 — Cost of Sources of Capital and WACC

The cost of capital is the return that providers of funds require.

Key points

  • Cost of equity (constant dividend): D / P. Growing dividend: D1 / P0 + g.
  • Cost of irredeemable debt after tax: interest x (1 - t) / price.
  • Redeemable debt cost is the IRR of its cash flows.
  • WACC = weighted average of the costs of equity, preference shares and debt, using market value weights.
  • WACC is used as the discount rate for investments.

Week 11 — Introduction to Dividend Policy

Dividend policy decides how much profit is paid out and how much is retained.

Key points

  • Retained earnings are not free: they have an opportunity cost.
  • Residual theory: invest in positive-NPV projects first, then pay dividends from what is left.
  • M&M irrelevance theory: in a perfect market dividend policy does not matter.
  • Traditional view: signalling effect and clientele effect make dividends matter.
  • Policies: constant payout ratio, stable dividend, zero dividend. Scrip dividend pays in shares.
